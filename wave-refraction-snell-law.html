Why do waves obey Snell’s law?

Water waves and light follow the same refraction geometry. Instead of memorizing two formulas, derive one general law from wavefronts and see why it also describes light.

Prerequisites: v = fλ, right-triangle trigonometry, wavefronts and rays. Model: a stationary, straight boundary between uniform, isotropic media; v denotes phase speed at the chosen frequency.

1. One law for waves

When a wave enters another medium, its frequency stays fixed by the source. Its phase speed may change, so its wavelength changes too. Measure both ray angles from the normal, not from the boundary.

sin θ₁ / v₁ = sin θ₂ / v₂

For nonzero incidence and a propagating transmitted wave, the equivalent ratio form is:

sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ = v₁ / v₂ = λ₁ / λ₂

A slower second medium gives a smaller angle and a shorter wavelength. A faster second medium gives a larger angle if a transmitted traveling wave exists. At normal incidence, both angles are zero; use the first form rather than dividing zero by zero.

2. Derive it with two right triangles

Imagine a straight wavefront AC approaching a boundary AB. Point A reaches the boundary first. During the same time Δt, point C advances to B in medium 1, while the wave from A advances a distance AD in medium 2. The new wavefront BD is tangent to the circular wavelet from A, so AD is perpendicular to BD.

  1. Same elapsed time. CB = v₁Δt and AD = v₂Δt.
  2. Same hypotenuse. In right triangle ACB, sin θ₁ = CB / AB = v₁Δt / L. In right triangle BDA, sin θ₂ = AD / AB = v₂Δt / L.
  3. Cancel the common factors. Divide each sine by its speed: sin θ₁ / v₁ = Δt / L = sin θ₂ / v₂.
  4. Use the wave equation. Since v₁ = fλ₁ and v₂ = fλ₂, their speed ratio equals their wavelength ratio. This is Snell’s law.

Equivalently, adjacent crests on both sides must meet the interface at the same positions: their common spacing along the interface is λ₁ / sin θ₁ = λ₂ / sin θ₂ (for oblique propagation). The wave cannot accumulate a different phase along the two sides of the same stationary boundary.

3. Explore refraction

Change the incident angle and speed ratio. Watch the crests turn while their intersections along the boundary remain matched. The incident wavelength is fixed; the transmitted wavelength scales with the speed ratio.

Refracted angle26.2°
Wavelength ratio0.625
DirectionToward normal

When v₂/v₁ · sin θ₁ exceeds 1, no real transmitted angle exists. An ideal interface may then exhibit total reflection with an evanescent field; the simulation omits that field and is not an amplitude or energy model.

4. Water and light: same mathematics

Water wave example

At 5.0 Hz, a wave travels from deep water (λ₁ = 4.0 cm) to shallow water (λ₂ = 2.5 cm). For θ₁ = 45°:

v₁ = 5.0 × 0.040 = 0.20 m/s
v₂ = 5.0 × 0.025 = 0.125 m/s
sin θ₂ = (0.125 / 0.20) sin 45°

θ₂ ≈ 26° — toward the normal.

Light example

For light, the refractive index is n = c/v, with c the speed of light in vacuum. Substitute v = c/n into the general law:

sin θ₁ / (c/n₁) = sin θ₂ / (c/n₂)

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

For air (n₁ ≈ 1.00) to glass (n₂ = 1.60), v₂/v₁ = n₁/n₂ = 0.625. At the same 45° incidence, the refracted angle is also about 26°. Different physical waves, identical geometric law at the same phase-speed ratio.

5. Check your understanding

Why does frequency remain unchanged?

The stationary boundary is driven by the incident wave at its source frequency. The transmitted wave oscillates at that same rate, even when its speed changes.

If the wave enters a faster medium at 30°, and v₂/v₁ = 1.5, what is θ₂?

sin θ₂ = 1.5 sin 30° = 0.75, so θ₂ ≈ 48.6°. It bends away from the normal.

At 0° incidence, does a change in speed bend the ray?

No. Both angles remain zero. Frequency is unchanged, but the wavelength changes in proportion to speed.

Why can you not measure wavelength along the boundary?

Wavelength is the perpendicular distance between adjacent crests. The spacing of crest intersections along the boundary is generally larger and equals λ/sin θ for oblique incidence.
