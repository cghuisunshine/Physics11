      2. Add the river’s 5.0 km/h downstream speed to get 15 km/h downstream relative to Earth.
      Question 2Textbook p. 261

      Answer

      1. 25 km/h left relative to the air.
      2. The bird’s Earth-frame velocity is 25 − 20 = 5 km/h left, the same as the river. It is at 0 km/h relative to the water.
      3. 5 km/h left relative to Earth. The wind blows 20 km/h to the right.
      Question 3Textbook p. 261

      Answer

      The river and bird both move left at 5 km/h relative to Earth. Subtract their velocities to get 0 km/h relative to the bird.

      Question 4Textbook p. 261

      Answer

      The boat moves left at 15 km/h relative to Earth and the bird at 5 km/h left. The person therefore moves 10 km/h downstream (left) relative to the bird.

      Question 5Textbook p. 261

      Answer

      The question describes a geostationary view, with the camera fixed over a point on the rotating Earth. In the Earth-fixed map used by the camera, the local ground is stationary, so the local Earth-relative velocities are unchanged. These are the boat at 15 km/h left, river and bird at 5 km/h left, and wind at 20 km/h right. The diagram’s boat-relative-to-water and bird-relative-to-air velocities describe the same pairwise motion.

      1 entries

      Question 8.1.1Textbook p. 267

      Answer

      With β = 0.500, γ = 1/√(1 − 0.500²) ≈ 1.1547. Thus Δt ≈ 1.155 Δτ for the same clock interval. An hour on board corresponds to about 69.3 min outside, about 9.3 min longer.

      Newtonian and relativistic momentum

      3 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 272

      Answer

      1. 0.10mc
      2. 0.50mc
      3. 0.87mc
      4. 0.9999mc
      5. mc

      These follow by formally substituting into p = mv. The Newtonian model is an approximation for low speeds and its c result is only a formal value.

      Question 2Textbook p. 272

      Answer

      Use p/(mc) = β/√(1 − β²), where m is the invariant mass. At c the formula has no finite value for an electron. The momentum grows without bound as v approaches c from below.

      Question 3Textbook p. 272

      Answer

      The average net force in the chosen frame is Δp/Δt. From rest, Δp = γmv. At any fixed finite time t, the required average force grows without bound as v approaches c. No finite force can bring an electron to c in finite time.

      Section review

      8.1 Review · Special relativity

      11 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 274

      Answer

      1. The laws of physics have the same form in all inertial frames.
      2. All inertial observers measure the same speed c for light in vacuum, regardless of the motion of its source.
      Question 2Textbook p. 274

      Answer

      The light passes at c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in the spaceship frame. Moving toward the star can change the frequency you measure.

      Question 3Textbook p. 274

      Answer

      1. The 10 a on the astronaut’s clock is proper time. Δt = 10/√(1 − 0.65²) ≈ 13.2 years in the Earth frame.
      2. L = (50.0 m)√(1 − 0.65²) ≈ 38.0 m. Measure the two endpoints simultaneously in the Earth frame.
      Question 4Textbook p. 275

      Answer

      With β = ½, Δt = t₀/√(1 − ¼) = 2t₀/√3 ≈ 1.155t₀. The bus clock measures the proper interval for an event pair at the same location in the bus.

      Question 5Textbook p. 275

      Answer

      Set L/L₀ = ½ = √(1 − β²). Then β² = ¾, so v = (√3/2)c ≈ 0.866c.

      Question 6Textbook p. 275

      Answer

      The ratio is 5 m/10 m = 0.5. Then v/c = √(1 − 0.5²) = 0.866. The bus travels at 0.866c.

      Question 7Textbook p. 275

      Answer

      Choice (c). The light has taken time to reach Earth, so it carries information about the star when the light was emitted. For example, a star 10 light-years away is seen roughly 10 years in the past in the Earth frame.

      Question 8Textbook p. 276

      Answer

      In the idealized model, Earth ages by 2520 − 2020 = 500 years. The traveller can spend at most 75 − 25 = 50 years on board, so γ must be at least 10. At γ = 10, v = c√(1 − 1/100) ≈ 0.995c. This assumes equal cruising speeds on the outward and return legs with negligible time spent turning around.

      Question 9Textbook p. 276

      Answer

      1 mg = 1 × 10⁻⁶ kg. E₀ = mc² ≈ (1 × 10⁻⁶)(3.00 × 10⁸)² = 9 × 10¹⁰ J if all of that mass is converted.

      Question 10Textbook p. 276

      Answer

      1. Convert 46 g to 0.046 kg. E₀ = (0.046)(3.00 × 10⁸)² ≈ 4.1 × 10¹⁵ J.
      2. 16 kWh/day = 5.76 × 10⁷ J/day. Dividing 4.14 × 10¹⁵ J by that rate gives about 7.19 × 10⁷ days, or 2.0 × 10⁵ years, under complete conversion and lossless storage.
      Question 11Textbook p. 277

      Answer

      Use your own monthly reading B in kWh. The monthly energy is B × 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Full conversion of 1 g gives (0.001)(3.00 × 10⁸)² = 9.0 × 10¹³ J, or 2.5 × 10⁷ kWh. Divide 2.5 × 10⁷ by B for the number of months. A reading of 600 kWh would give about 4.2 × 10⁴ months.

      Extension

      8.1 Extension · The speed limit and gravity

      2 entries

      Question 12Textbook p. 277

      Answer

      For invariant mass m, p = γmv. As v approaches c, γ increases without bound and so does the required momentum. Total energy E = γmc² also grows without bound. An electron can be accelerated very close to c, but cannot reach c with finite energy.

      Question 13Textbook p. 277

      Answer

      Special relativity describes physics in flat spacetime. It can also describe an accelerating object by tracking its motion in that spacetime. General relativity describes gravity through spacetime curvature produced by matter and energy. Freely falling objects follow geodesics, and clocks can accumulate different proper times when they follow different paths or remain at different heights in a gravitational field.
