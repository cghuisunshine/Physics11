      Quick check

      The discovery of radioactivity

      3 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 282

      Answer

      Becquerel was testing whether a fluorescent uranium compound emitted penetrating radiation like X-rays. The wrapped photographic plate darkened even when the compound had been stored in the dark. The emission came from the material itself.

      Question 2Textbook p. 282

      Answer

      Marie Curie introduced the term radioactivity for the spontaneous emission.

      Question 3Textbook p. 282

      Answer

      Ernest Rutherford distinguished and named alpha and beta radiation by their different penetration. Paul Villard detected the third, more penetrating component later called gamma radiation. Experiments later established what each component was.

      Quick check

      Identifying alpha, beta, and gamma radiation

      3 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 284

      Answer

      For a beam crossing the field, positive alpha and negative beta-minus particles deflect in opposite directions. Gamma photons have zero charge and travel straight in the field. The radius of a charged track also depends on its momentum, charge, and field strength.

      Question 2Textbook p. 284

      Answer

      Henri Becquerel used deflection measurements to determine the charge-to-mass ratio of beta particles. Its negative sign and magnitude matched those of electrons measured in cathode rays.

      Question 3Textbook p. 284

      Answer

      Rutherford and Edward Andrade used crystal diffraction. The crystal produced a diffraction pattern, as it does for X-rays, supporting the interpretation of gamma radiation as short-wavelength electromagnetic radiation.

      Quick check

      Transmutation and the nucleus

      4 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 287

      Answer

      Natural transmutation is the spontaneous change of one element into another when a nucleus decays and its proton number changes. Alpha and beta-minus decay can do this. Gamma emission leaves the element unchanged.

      Question 2Textbook p. 287

      Answer

      Rutherford pictured hydrogen as a very small, dense, positive nucleus containing a single proton, with one negative electron outside it. Most of the atom’s volume lies outside the nucleus. Modern models describe the electron with a quantum state.

      Question 3Textbook p. 287

      Answer

      A proton is a positively charged constituent of a nucleus. The atomic number Z counts its protons and determines the element.

      Question 4Textbook p. 287

      Answer

      A free neutron is unstable. It undergoes beta-minus decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ. Its mean lifetime is about 15 minutes. Nuclear binding can make a neutron stable within a stable nucleus.

      Isotopes and nuclear equations

      4 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 290

      Answer

      Isotopes are nuclei of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. They have different mass numbers and may differ in nuclear stability. Neutral atoms of the same element have the same number of electrons.

      Question 2Textbook p. 290

      Answer

      A radioisotope is an isotope with an unstable nucleus that can decay spontaneously.

      Question 3Textbook p. 290

      Answer

      ²³⁸₉₂U is uranium with 92 protons and 238 nucleons. It contains 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons. A neutral atom has 92 electrons.

      Question 4Textbook p. 290

      Answer

      1. ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He. The printed radon subscript is 88. Radon has Z = 86, which balances the equation with the emitted alpha particle.
      2. ²¹⁴₈₂Pb → ²¹⁴₈₃Bi + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ. The missing charged particle is the beta-minus electron.
      3. ²¹⁸₈₄Po → ²¹⁴₈₂Pb + ⁴₂He. The daughter is lead-214.
      4. ²¹⁰₈₃Bi → ²¹⁰₈₄Po + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ. The daughter is polonium-210.

      Question 1Textbook p. 294

      Answer

      Take half the combined initial count and read where the class curve crosses that level. Record the number of rolls, interpolating between adjacent points if useful. The observed value depends on the class data. For fair dice the expected fraction after n rolls is (5/6)ⁿ, so the model half-life is ln(1/2)/ln(5/6) ≈ 3.8 rolls.

      Section review

      9.1 Review · Radioactivity

      7 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      Becquerel’s radiation was invisible, passed through paper that blocked visible light, fogged photographic plates, and ionized air. The emission persisted without prior exposure to light. Different components later showed different penetration.

      Question 2Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      Transmutation showed that radioactivity could change the identity of an element. A change in proton number comes from a nuclear process. The finding helped explain the appearance of daughter substances in a decaying sample.

      Question 3Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      James Chadwick studied the neutral radiation produced when alpha particles struck beryllium. It knocked protons out of hydrogen-rich material such as paraffin. Recoil measurements supported a neutral particle with a mass close to that of the proton.

      Question 4Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      The alpha particle is thousands of times more massive than an electron. An electron can give it only a small deflection. The observed large-angle scattering is explained by close approaches to a concentrated positive nucleus.

      Question 5Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      A half-life is the time for the expected number of undecayed parent nuclei to fall to half its initial value. The activity of that parent isotope also halves if no additional parent nuclei are supplied.

      Question 6Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      1. ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ. Nitrogen-14 has Z = 7. The electron must have charge number −1 to balance 6 = 7 − 1. The source shows a positive subscript.
      2. ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂He. The missing particle is ⁴₂He.
      3. ²²⁵₈₉Ac → ²²¹₈₇Fr + ⁴₂He. The parent has A = 225 and Z = 89.
      4. ²⁰⁹₈₂Pb → ²⁰⁹₈₃Bi + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ. The parent is lead-209 and the emitted charged particle is a beta-minus electron.
      Question 7Textbook p. 295

      Answer

      The fraction of parent carbon-14 left is (1/2)⁵ = 1/32. 4000 g/32 = 125 g of carbon-14. The remaining material can include decay products.

      Fission and fusion

      1 entries

      Question Warm-upTextbook p. 296

      Answer

      Fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter fragments. Fusion joins light nuclei to make a heavier nucleus. Both can release energy when the total product rest mass is lower.

      Section review

      9.2 Review · Nuclear fission and fusion

      6 entries

      Question 1Textbook p. 302

      Answer

      1. Cockcroft and Walton used accelerated protons to induce a reaction in lithium-7. The products were two helium-4 nuclei.
      2. The products had lower total rest mass. The mass difference released energy according to Q = (m(initial) − m(final))c², mostly as kinetic energy of the helium nuclei in this reaction.
      Question 2Textbook p. 302

      Answer

      1. Fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into lighter fragments, often after neutron absorption.
      2. It releases a large amount of energy, mainly first as kinetic energy of the fragments. Additional neutrons can also be emitted.
      3. Some emitted neutrons can cause further uranium-235 fissions, so the reaction can sustain itself.
      Question 3Textbook p. 302

      Answer

      A uranium-235 fission generally emits several neutrons. If, on average, one causes another fission, the chain reaction is self-sustaining at steady power. The other neutrons can escape or be absorbed without producing fission.

      Question 5Textbook p. 302

      Answer

      BenefitsTrade-offs
      High energy yield from a relatively small amount of fuel. Low direct greenhouse-gas emissions during electricity generation.Used fuel and other radioactive waste require isolation and long-term management.
      Steady electricity generation while the plant is operating.Construction and decommissioning require substantial resources. Safety and cooling systems need continuing oversight.
      Question 6Textbook p. 302

      Answer

      The reactor controls the chain reaction by regulating how many neutrons produce new fissions. Neutron absorbers and independent shutdown systems can reduce that number. Coolant removes heat, including decay heat after shutdown, and containment helps limit releases. A conventional reactor is designed for controlled, steady operation.

      Question 1Textbook p. 303

      Answer

      Becquerel placed a uranium compound near a light-shielded photographic plate while studying fluorescence. The plate was exposed even when the compound had been kept in the dark. The observation revealed spontaneous radiation from the compound.

      Question 2Textbook p. 303

      Answer

      Rutherford’s model placed positive charge and most of the mass in a tiny nucleus. Most of the atom’s volume was outside the nucleus. The negative electrons occupied the surrounding region.

      Question 3Textbook p. 303

      Answer

      Early researchers observed effects such as skin burns as they worked with radioactive sources, but dose measurement and knowledge of long-term health effects were limited. Radiation protection developed as these effects became better understood.

      Question 4Textbook p. 303

      Answer

      In this chapter a beta particle is a beta-minus electron, with negative charge and nonzero rest mass. A gamma ray is a photon emitted by the nucleus. It has zero charge and zero rest mass.

      Question 5Textbook p. 303

      Answer

      ⁰₋₁e is an electron, written this way when it is emitted in beta-minus decay. Its mass number is zero because it contains no nucleons. The lower −1 is its charge number.

      Question 6Textbook p. 303

      Answer

      Both isotopes have 92 protons. Uranium-235 has 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons, while uranium-238 has 238 − 92 = 146 neutrons. Their mass numbers and nuclear properties differ.

      Question 7Textbook p. 304

      1. ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He.
      2. ²³⁴₉₀Th → ²³⁴₉₁Pa + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ.
      Question 8Textbook p. 304

      Answer

      The half-life is the time for the number of undecayed parent nuclei, or their activity, to fall to half its starting value. Repeat the halving for each additional half-life.

It gives a measurable signal during imaging and leaves less lingering activity afterward. The actual choice also depends on how the tracer behaves in the body.

      2. ²³⁴₉₀Th → ²³⁴₉₁Pa + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ. The missing charged particle is a beta-minus electron.
      3. ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ. The missing daughter is nitrogen-14.
      4. ²¹⁸₈₄Po → ²¹⁴₈₂Pb + ⁴₂He. The missing particle is an alpha particle.
      Question 12Textbook p. 304

      Answer

      Fuel contains nuclei that undergo fission. A moderator slows neutrons in a thermal reactor. Control and shutdown absorbers capture neutrons. Coolant carries away heat. Shielding and containment limit radiation exposure and releases. In a power station, steam drives a turbine and generator, and a condenser returns the steam to water.

      Question 14Textbook p. 305

      Answer

      Fusion joins light nuclei and can release energy. Example: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + energy. Deuterium and tritium are isotopes of hydrogen.

      Question 15Textbook p. 305

      Answer

      Positive nuclei repel one another electrically. A fusion system must bring them close enough for the nuclear force to bind them. It needs a very hot plasma and enough density and confinement time for useful numbers of collisions, while limiting energy losses and handling the reaction products.

      Question 16Textbook p. 305

      Answer

      Fusion could use abundant deuterium and would avoid a self-sustaining fission chain reaction. D–T fusion still requires tritium management and produces neutrons that can activate materials. Practical energy generation depends on meeting confinement, materials, maintenance, and energy-balance requirements.

      Question 17Textbook p. 305

      Answer

      Light nuclei such as hydrogen isotopes can fuse and release energy. They need sufficient relative energy to approach despite electric repulsion, plus enough density and confinement time for a useful reaction rate. In stars, gravity provides confinement.

      Answer

      Absorber tests showed components with different penetrating abilities. A transverse field also separated the positive and negative components while a neutral component continued straight. These observations distinguished alpha, beta, and gamma radiation.

      The residual strong nuclear force binds nearby nucleons and can overcome proton–proton electric repulsion at nuclear distances. It acts over roughly a few femtometres, of order 10⁻¹⁵ m, and falls off rapidly beyond that range.

      Question 11Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      A uranium-235 nucleus can absorb a neutron and form an excited uranium-236 nucleus. It can then split into two lighter fragments and release energy plus additional neutrons. The fragments transfer kinetic energy to their surroundings as heat. The released neutrons can cause further fissions.

      Question 12Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      Fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter ones. Fusion joins light nuclei. Either can release energy if the final products have less total rest mass.

      Question 13Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      1. Uranium-238 can capture a slow neutron to form uranium-239. Slow-neutron capture usually does not cause U-238 to fission.
      2. Uranium-235 can absorb a slow neutron and undergo fission, releasing energy, lighter fragments, and more neutrons. Some captures can occur without fission.
      Question 14Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      Critical mass is the amount of fissile material needed for a self-sustaining chain reaction under specified conditions. In a nuclear weapon, the fission stage is brought from a subcritical state to a rapidly supercritical state, so successive neutron generations grow very quickly.

      Question 15Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      A thermonuclear weapon uses energy from a fission stage to create the extreme conditions for light nuclei to fuse. The high temperature lets some nuclei approach closely enough to react.

      Question 16Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      Fusion could provide useful energy from light nuclei, including deuterium. It offers a high energy yield per mass of fuel. A practical system must sustain the plasma and capture enough of the released energy while coping with losses and material damage.

      Question 17Textbook p. 306

      Answer

      1. Most stars consist mainly of hydrogen and helium.
      2. Nuclear fusion powers stars. For much of a star’s life, fusion converts hydrogen into helium.
