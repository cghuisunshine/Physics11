1 Pulses and periodic waves

How a wave carries energy

A wave is a disturbance that travels and transfers energy. Give a rope one sideways flick to launch a pulse. Move the rope end back and forth steadily to launch a periodic wave. The pattern travels down the rope while each segment oscillates around its equilibrium position.

A mechanical wave, such as a sound wave, needs matter to transmit the disturbance. An electromagnetic wave, such as visible light or a radio wave, can travel through a vacuum.

Warm-up 6.1 · A singing glass

A damp finger rubbed around a glass rim can make it ring. Compare the pitch at two fill levels. Can an empty glass ring?

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

A rubbed empty glass can ring because its walls vibrate and disturb the air. Adding water generally lowers its resonant frequency and pitch because some water moves with the vibrating glass. Use the same glass and rubbing technique in each trial.

2 Amplitude, wavelength, and phase

Reading a wave snapshot

A crest is a maximum upward displacement and a trough is a maximum downward displacement. Measure the amplitude A vertically from equilibrium to a crest or trough. Measure wavelength λ along the direction of travel between consecutive points with the same phase, such as adjacent crests.

Points one wavelength apart in a sinusoidal travelling wave have the same displacement and are moving through their cycles in step. Points half a wavelength apart are half a cycle out of phase. Two points can have the same displacement and be moving in opposite directions.

Example · From a displacement–position graph

Two consecutive troughs are at x = 0.30 m and x = 0.90 m. Each trough is 4.0 cm below equilibrium. Find λ, amplitude, and the vertical trough-to-crest height.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

λ = 0.90 − 0.30 = 0.60 m
A = 4.0 cm = 0.040 m
Trough-to-crest height = 2A = 8.0 cm

The horizontal axis gives distances. A displacement–time graph at one point would give the period on its horizontal axis.

3 Frequency and period

Count cycles and measure time

Frequency f counts cycles per second, in hertz. Period T is the time for one complete cycle, in seconds. Count complete cycles and divide by the total time, or time many cycles and divide by the count to get the average period.

f = N/Δt = 1/T             T = Δt/N = 1/f
1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹               1 MHz = 10⁶ Hz

On a time graph, take the time between adjacent crests or between successive equilibrium crossings in the same direction. A single back-and-forth swing is one cycle.

Quick Check 6.1 · Counting vibrations

A tuning fork makes 7680 vibrations in 30 s. Find its frequency and period.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

f = 7680/30 = 256 Hz
T = 1/256 = 0.00390625 s ≈ 3.91 ms

A 256 Hz source produces 256 cycles each second. Each cycle takes about 3.91 milliseconds.

4 Transverse and longitudinal waves

Compare the two directions

For a transverse wave, the medium oscillates perpendicular to the propagation direction. A pulse on a taut rope can move horizontally while a marked segment moves vertically.

For a longitudinal wave, the medium oscillates parallel to the propagation direction. In a spring, a bunched region is a compression and a spread-out region is a rarefaction. Wavelength can be measured from one compression centre to the next. Ordinary sound in air is longitudinal.

Investigation 6-1A · Pulses in springs

Send a sideways pulse and a compression pulse along the same stretched spring. Track a marked coil, then time the pulse over a fixed distance. Compare different tensions and small amplitudes. Hold the far end fixed and observe the reflected transverse pulse.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

The marked coil moves across the spring for a transverse pulse and along it for a longitudinal pulse. In each case the coil returns near its starting point. Higher tension generally makes transverse pulses faster. Small changes of amplitude at fixed tension have little effect on speed. A fixed-end transverse reflection inverts the pulse. Use your measured travel times to compare different springs.

5 The universal wave equation

Why one wavelength takes one period

During one source cycle, a travelling crest moves one wavelength. Divide that distance by the time for the cycle.

v = λ/T = fλ
λ = v/f                 f = v/λ
v in m/s                λ in m                f in Hz

Use the speed of the wave pattern. At fixed speed, doubling the frequency halves the wavelength. Convert length and time into matching units before substituting. Light travels at about 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum.

Sample 6.1.1 · Speed of sound

A sound wave has frequency 256 Hz and wavelength 1.29 m. Find its speed.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

v = (256 Hz)(1.29 m) = 330.24 m/s ≈ 330 m/s

Example · Finding distance with a reflected pulse

A sonar pulse travels at 1500 m/s and returns to its source after 2.40 s. Find the distance to the reflecting surface.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

The time covers the outward and return legs. Distance to the surface = vΔt/2 = (1500)(2.40)/2 = 1800 m.

6 Measuring water waves

Reading the ripple tank

A point source makes circular wavefronts in uniform water. A straight bar that dips uniformly into the water can generate nearly straight fronts. Overhead lighting projects the water pattern onto a screen. In the textbook arrangement, crests concentrate light into bright lines. Those lines locate the crests. They are an optical projection of the water motion.

Investigation 6-1B · Relating speed, wavelength, and frequency

At fixed depth, vary the generator frequency. Measure a span of several consecutive bright lines, divide by the number of gaps, and calculate v = fλ. Use the same screen scale and account for projection magnification if converting screen lengths to actual tank distances.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

If five gaps span 0.200 m in the tank, λ = 0.200/5 = 0.0400 m. At 5.00 Hz, v = 0.200 m/s. At 10.0 Hz and the same speed, λ would be 0.0200 m. If your calculated speeds differ beyond measurement uncertainty, the constant-speed model may be too simple. Water-wave speed can depend on wavelength as well as depth.

7 Reflection

Flat and curved boundaries

Reflection sends part or all of a wave back into its original medium. Draw the normal perpendicular to the barrier. A ray shows the direction of travel and is perpendicular to the wavefront. The reflected ray makes the same angle with the normal as the incoming ray.

Angle of reflection = angle of incidence
Same medium after reflection → same v, f, and λ

A circular reflection from a flat barrier appears to come from a virtual source equally far behind it. A parabolic reflector directs incoming parallel rays along its axis toward a focus. Putting a point source at the focus reverses the process and gives nearly parallel reflected rays.

Investigation 6-2 · Stations 1–3

In a ripple tank, compare reflected circular fronts, measure the reflected angle of a straight front, and send straight waves toward a parabolic barrier. A ray strikes a flat barrier at 35° to the normal. How does it leave?

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

The reflected ray leaves at 35° to the normal on the other side of the normal. A straight wavefront makes the same angle with the barrier as its ray makes with the normal. In the curved setup, straight fronts incident along the axis converge toward the focus.

8 Refraction

Across a boundary

At a stationary boundary, the source fixes the frequency on both sides. A change of speed therefore changes wavelength. For oblique incidence, a wave entering a slower region bends toward the normal. A wave entering a faster region bends away from the normal. At normal incidence, wavelength and speed may change while the direction stays straight.

v₂/v₁ = λ₂/λ₁              f₂ = f₁

Investigation 6-2 · Station 4

Measure crest spacing in deep and shallow water. Repeat with the boundary turned so the incoming fronts meet it obliquely. Suppose the wavelength drops from 4.0 cm to 2.5 cm at 5.0 Hz. Find both speeds and describe the turn.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

v(deep) = (5.0)(0.040) = 0.20 m/s
v(shallow) = (5.0)(0.025) = 0.125 m/s ≈ 0.13 m/s
v(shallow)/v(deep) = 2.5/4.0 ≈ 0.63

The ray turns toward the normal. It bends away on re-entering deep water. Parallel entry and exit boundaries with equal depths outside the shallow strip restore the original direction, with a possible sideways offset.

9 Diffraction

Waves spread past edges

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave around an obstacle or beyond an opening. When an opening is about a wavelength wide or smaller, the outgoing wavefronts spread widely and can look nearly semicircular. When the opening is many wavelengths wide, the wave remains more nearly straight except near the edges.

Sound can reach a listener around a doorway because typical audible wavelengths can be comparable to the opening. Visible-light wavelengths are much smaller than a doorway, so spreading there is usually too slight to notice. Fine mesh can diffract visible light.

Investigation 6-2 · Station 5

Keep the water-wave wavelength at 2.0 cm. Compare a 2.0 cm opening with a 10 cm opening, then remove the barrier and compare obstacles of different widths.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

The 2.0 cm opening gives wider spreading because its width is comparable to λ. Behind a large obstacle there is a clearer shadow, with bending around the edges. A very small obstacle produces little persistent shadow because waves spread around it.

10 Superposition and interference

Add signed displacements

When waves overlap in a linear medium, add their instantaneous displacements. Constructive interference reinforces a displacement. Destructive interference reduces it. Complete cancellation requires opposite displacements of equal size. In the ideal model each pulse continues after the overlap.

Resultant displacement = y₁ + y₂

Two sources are coherent when their phase difference stays fixed. For coherent sources oscillating in phase, matching path lengths give a maximum. So does a path difference of a whole number of wavelengths. Half-integer multiples give minima, including nodal lines when the amplitudes match.

Investigation 6-2 · Station 6

Send two equal pulses along a spring, first with the same sign and then with opposite signs. In the ripple tank, compare points with path differences of 1.0λ and 1.5λ from two in-phase sources.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

Equal upward pulses of height A give 2A at exact overlap. An upward and equal downward pulse give zero during exact overlap. In the tank, 1.0λ gives constructive interference. 1.5λ gives destructive interference because the extra travel is one and a half cycles.

11 Young’s double slit

From ripple patterns to light

Two narrow slits fed by the same wave can act as coherent sources. Each slit diffracts the wave. The overlaps produce bright bands where the fields reinforce and dark bands where they cancel. For two in-phase slits and a distant screen, the path difference in direction θ is approximately d sin θ, where d is the slit separation.

Bright band of order m:        d sin θ = mλ
Dark band:                     d sin θ = (m + ½)λ
For small θ, bright bands:     y ≈ mλL/d

The central bright band has m = 0. L is the perpendicular distance to the screen, and y is the offset from the centre. Use θ measured from the line pointing straight toward the centre of the screen.

Example · Wavelength from a fringe pattern

Two slits are 0.250 mm apart. On a screen 2.00 m away, the first bright band is 4.80 mm from the centre. Find λ using the small-angle relation.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

λ ≈ yd/(mL) = (4.80 × 10⁻³)(2.50 × 10⁻⁴)/(1 × 2.00)
  = 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ m = 600 nm

The small-angle approximation fits here because y/L = 0.00240.

12 Doppler effect and sonic booms

Motion changes the rate of arrival

The Doppler effect is a change in observed frequency due to motion of the source or observer. A moving source emits each new crest from a different position. In still air and below the speed of sound, fronts crowd ahead of the source and spread behind it. A moving observer can also encounter fronts at a different rate. Approaching motion along the line of travel raises observed pitch. Receding motion lowers it.

The sound barrier and the shock

At about Mach 1, compressibility and shock effects become important. A supersonic source outruns its own sound and produces a shock cone. An observer hears a sonic boom when the shock passes. The Mach number is source speed divided by the local speed of sound.

Example · Two listeners and a moving source

A vehicle sounding a steady horn passes between two stationary listeners, one ahead and one behind. Compare the pitch and wave speed each hears while it is still moving toward the first listener.

Show answerHide answer

Worked answer

The listener ahead hears a higher pitch because the fronts are closer together. The listener behind hears a lower pitch because the fronts are farther apart. Sound propagates at the same speed relative to still air in both directions. The horn’s emitted frequency stays fixed.
