## 8.1 Generating waves: energy and medium motion

### Learning goal
Distinguish transverse and longitudinal waves and separate wave travel from particle motion.

### Understand the model
A mechanical wave is a disturbance propagated through an interacting medium. The disturbance carries energy, while particles of the medium oscillate about equilibrium rather than travelling from source to receiver with the wave in the basic model. A rope marker moves up and down while the pulse travels along the rope. Sound in air involves back-and-forth particle motion and alternating compressions and rarefactions.

A transverse wave has particle motion perpendicular to propagation; a longitudinal wave has particle motion parallel to propagation. A single pulse differs from a repeating periodic wave. Straight wavefronts describe a locally plane wave; circular wavefronts spread from a source in a two-dimensional medium. Rays indicate propagation direction and are perpendicular to wavefronts in a uniform isotropic medium.

### Equations and units
```text
transverse: particle motion ⟂ propagation
longitudinal: particle motion ∥ propagation
wavefront = points at the same phase
```

### Worked example
A pulse travels 3.0 m along a rope in 0.50 s. A marked point rises 0.040 m and returns. Compare wave speed and marker displacement.

1. Wave speed is 3.0/0.50 = 6.0 m/s along the rope.
2. The marker’s motion is transverse, not 3.0 m along the rope. Its final displacement is zero after the pulse passes in the simple model.
3. The wave carried energy through the rope even though the marker returned to its original position.

### Common mistakes
A wave’s speed is not the same as the instantaneous speed of one particle of the medium. Water surface waves involve more complex particle motion than a perfectly transverse rope model.

### Your turn
1. What makes sound in air longitudinal?
2. Can mechanical sound travel through an ideal vacuum?

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** Air particles oscillate parallel to the direction in which compressions and rarefactions travel.

**2.** No. Mechanical sound requires a material medium with particles that interact.

## 8.2 Amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, and speed

### Learning goal
Read wave properties from spatial and time graphs and use v = fλ.

### Understand the model
Amplitude A is the maximum displacement from equilibrium. Wavelength λ is the distance between neighboring points in the same phase, such as crest to crest in a sinusoidal snapshot. Period T is the time for one oscillation at a fixed location, and frequency f counts oscillations per second. One hertz means one cycle per second.

In one period a travelling periodic pattern advances one wavelength, giving v = λ/T = fλ. The source sets frequency while the medium and its conditions determine wave speed. A displacement-position graph shows wavelength; a displacement-time graph at one point shows period. A change in amplitude alone does not change speed in the linear ideal model. For a stretched string, speed increases with tension and decreases with linear mass density.

### Equations and units
```text
f = 1/T
v = fλ = λ/T
string wave speed = √(F_tension/μ_linear)
μ_linear = string mass/string length
```

### Worked example
A wave has wavelength 0.80 m and frequency 5.0 Hz. Find period and speed. It enters a medium where its speed is 2.0 m/s.

1. T = 1/5.0 = 0.20 s.
2. v = (5.0)(0.80) = 4.0 m/s in the first medium.
3. At a stationary boundary the transmitted frequency stays 5.0 Hz, so the new wavelength is 2.0/5.0 = 0.40 m.

### Common mistakes
A crest-to-trough vertical separation is 2A, not A. A crest-to-next-trough horizontal separation is λ/2, not λ.

### Your turn
1. A wave period is 0.025 s and speed is 12 m/s. Find f and λ.
2. String tension increases by a factor of four with unchanged linear density. How does wave speed change?

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** f = 40 Hz; λ = v/f = 0.30 m.

**2.** Speed doubles because v is proportional to the square root of tension.

## 8.3 Reflection, refraction, transmission, and diffraction

### Learning goal
Predict wave behavior at a boundary or opening and state which quantities change.

### Understand the model
At a boundary, part of a wave can reflect and part can transmit. A transverse pulse reflected from a fixed end inverts; at a free end it reflects without inversion. Boundary behavior depends on how the medium is constrained. For sound, distinguish displacement patterns from pressure patterns instead of transferring rope rules blindly.

Refraction changes propagation direction when wave speed changes across a boundary and incidence is not normal. Frequency stays fixed by the source at a stationary boundary, so wavelength changes with speed. Diffraction is spreading around obstacles or through openings, and is more pronounced when an opening or obstacle size is comparable with wavelength. These boundary ideas extend the source’s wave and interference treatment to the curriculum’s qualitative wave behaviors.

### Equations and units
```text
at a stationary boundary: f₁ = f₂
λ₂/λ₁ = v₂/v₁
refraction: sin θ₁/sin θ₂ = v₁/v₂
angles measured from the normal
```

### Worked example
A wave enters a slower medium at an angle. Its speed drops from 4.0 to 2.0 m/s while frequency remains 5.0 Hz. Explain wavelength and direction.

1. The original wavelength is 4.0/5.0 = 0.80 m and the transmitted wavelength is 2.0/5.0 = 0.40 m.
2. The refraction relation gives a smaller angle from the normal in the slower medium, so the ray bends toward the normal.
3. For normal incidence there is no bending, although wavelength still changes. Boundary conditions may also produce a reflected wave.

### Common mistakes
Refraction angles are measured from the normal, not the surface. “Frequency stays the same” assumes a stationary boundary and the same driving source.

### Your turn
1. What happens to a rope pulse reflected at a fixed end?
2. Which opening produces stronger spreading for the same wave: much wider than λ or comparable to λ?

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** Its transverse displacement inverts: an upward incident pulse reflects as a downward pulse.

**2.** An opening comparable to λ generally produces more pronounced diffraction.

## 8.4 Superposition and interference

### Learning goal
Add simultaneous displacements and relate path difference to reinforcement or cancellation.

### Understand the model
In a linear medium, superposition says the resultant displacement at a point is the algebraic sum of the individual wave displacements. Crest meeting crest produces constructive interference; crest meeting trough can produce destructive interference. Equal opposite displacements cancel at that instant, but the waves can continue beyond each other. Local cancellation does not mean the whole system’s energy vanishes.

For two coherent sources initially in phase, a path difference of an integer number of wavelengths gives constructive interference, and a half-integer gives destructive interference. The phase relationship matters: initially out-of-phase sources reverse those conditions. Stable interference requires a consistent relative phase; unrelated changing sources do not make a fixed pattern.

### Equations and units
```text
y_resultant = y₁ + y₂
in-phase sources: constructive Δr = nλ
in-phase sources: destructive Δr = (n + ½)λ
n = 0, 1, 2, ...
```

### Worked example
At a point, two pulses have displacements +3.0 cm and -1.0 cm. Separately, two in-phase sources have λ = 0.40 m and path difference 0.60 m.

1. Pulse displacement adds algebraically: +3.0 + (-1.0) = +2.0 cm.
2. The path difference ratio is 0.60/0.40 = 1.5 wavelengths.
3. This is a half-integer, so the coherent in-phase waves interfere destructively there. Complete cancellation also requires equal amplitudes.

### Common mistakes
Interference depends on phase, not just whether sources are nearby. “Destructive” does not guarantee zero amplitude when the interfering amplitudes differ.

### Your turn
1. Two identical +2.0 cm pulses overlap completely. Find resultant displacement.
2. In-phase sources have path difference 2λ. Is interference constructive?

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** y = +4.0 cm during the overlap in the linear model.

**2.** Yes. The arrival phases differ by two complete cycles.

## 8.5 Standing waves, resonance, and string harmonics

### Learning goal
Locate nodes and antinodes and determine the allowed frequencies of a fixed-end string.

### Understand the model
A standing wave forms from interference of travelling waves moving in opposite directions. Nodes remain at zero displacement; antinodes have the largest oscillation amplitude. Neighboring nodes are separated by λ/2, and a node and its neighboring antinode by λ/4. The pattern appears stationary even though the medium oscillates between nodes.

A string fixed at both ends must have displacement nodes at both ends. Its length contains n half-wavelengths, so λ_n = 2L/n and f_n = nv/(2L). The lowest frequency is the fundamental; higher integer multiples are harmonics. Resonance occurs when a periodic drive matches a natural frequency, allowing substantial energy transfer and a large response. Damping limits the amplitude of real resonance.

### Equations and units
```text
fixed ends: L = nλ_n/2
λ_n = 2L/n
f_n = nv/(2L), n = 1, 2, 3, ...
node spacing = λ/2
```

### Worked example
A string is 0.800 m long and wave speed is 120 m/s. Find the first three harmonic frequencies and the second-harmonic wavelength.

1. Fundamental f₁ = 120/[2(0.800)] = 75.0 Hz, using the assigned speed value.
2. f₂ = 150 Hz and f₃ = 225 Hz. The corresponding patterns have two and three half-wavelength segments.
3. λ₂ = 2(0.800)/2 = 0.800 m. The second harmonic has a node at the middle as well as at both fixed ends.

### Common mistakes
A standing wave’s antinode is a location, not a crest permanently frozen in place. For equal counter-propagating waves the ideal standing pattern has no net average energy flow along the string.

### Your turn
1. A fixed-end string has f₁ = 60 Hz. Find f₄.
2. Nodes are 0.25 m apart. Find wavelength.

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** f₄ = 4f₁ = 240 Hz.

**2.** Neighboring node separation is λ/2, so λ = 0.50 m.

## 8.6 Sound speed, pitch, intensity, and sound level

### Learning goal
Connect frequency to pitch, intensity to sound level, and travel time to distance.

### Understand the model
Sound in air is a longitudinal mechanical wave. Use 343 m/s for air at about 20 °C when assigned; temperature and the medium affect the speed. Pitch is strongly associated with frequency. Loudness is a perceptual response influenced by intensity, frequency, and the listener, so it is not identical to amplitude or a decibel number.

Intensity is power passing through unit area. An ideal point source spreading uniformly in three dimensions gives I = P/(4πr²). Doubling distance then reduces intensity to one quarter. Sound level is logarithmic: a tenfold intensity increase adds 10 dB, while doubling intensity adds about 3 dB. For a fixed medium and frequency in the linear model, intensity scales with squared displacement amplitude.

### Equations and units
```text
sound speed in air at 20 °C: v ≈ 343 m/s
I = P/A; point source I = P/(4πr²)
β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) dB
I₀ = 1.0 × 10⁻¹² W/m²
echo distance = vt_round-trip/2
```

### Worked example
An echo returns after 0.800 s in air at 20 °C. Find reflecting-wall distance. A sound has intensity 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ W/m²; find sound level.

1. The sound travels to the wall and back, so d = (343)(0.800)/2 = 137 m.
2. I/I₀ = 10⁶, so β = 10 log₁₀(10⁶) = 60 dB.
3. A tenfold intensity increase would make 70 dB; it would not multiply the decibel reading by ten.

### Common mistakes
Do not omit the factor of two for an echo. A higher amplitude changes intensity rather than frequency in the ideal model. A given dB increase is not a universal loudness multiplier.

### Your turn
1. An ideal point-source intensity is measured at r and 2r. Compare the values.
2. Intensity increases from I to 100I. How much does β rise?

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** I(2r) = I(r)/4, assuming the same source and no additional absorption or reflection.

**2.** Δβ = 10 log₁₀(100) = 20 dB.

## 8.7 Air-column resonance and beats

### Learning goal
Distinguish open and closed pipe harmonics and calculate beat frequency.

### Understand the model
For air-particle displacement, an open end is approximately an antinode and a closed rigid end a node. Pressure nodes and antinodes are reversed. An open-open pipe supports integer harmonics with f_n = nv/(2L). A pipe closed at one end and open at the other fits odd quarter-wavelength patterns, so f_n = nv/(4L) for n = 1, 3, 5, and so on. These simple formulas neglect end corrections.

Two nearby frequencies interfere to produce beats: a slow variation of amplitude with frequency |f₁ - f₂|. Beats help compare or tune sound sources. The beat frequency gives the magnitude of their frequency difference but alone does not show which source is higher. Resonance and beats are different phenomena: resonance involves a driven natural mode, while beats arise from overlapping nearby frequencies.

### Equations and units
```text
open-open pipe: f_n = nv/(2L), n = 1, 2, 3, ...
open-closed pipe: f_n = nv/(4L), n = 1, 3, 5, ...
f_beat = |f₁ - f₂|
```

### Worked example
An open-closed pipe has L = 0.500 m in air with v = 343 m/s. Find its first two allowed frequencies. Compare 440 Hz and 444 Hz tones.

1. Fundamental f₁ = 343/[4(0.500)] = 171.5 Hz ≈ 172 Hz.
2. The next allowed frequency is f₃ = 3f₁ = 514.5 Hz ≈ 515 Hz. The n = 2 harmonic is absent in this ideal pipe.
3. The two tones produce |444 - 440| = 4 Hz beats, meaning four amplitude cycles per second.

### Common mistakes
For an open-closed pipe, the second allowed mode is the third harmonic. Say whether a node refers to particle displacement or pressure.

### Your turn
1. An open-open pipe has fundamental 200 Hz. Find its next two harmonic frequencies.
2. A 256 Hz fork beats at 3 Hz against another fork. What frequencies might the other fork have?

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** 400 Hz and 600 Hz.

**2.** 253 Hz or 259 Hz. More evidence is needed to decide which.

## 8.8 Doppler shifts and sonic booms

### Learning goal
Predict frequency changes from relative motion and distinguish a sonic boom from ordinary Doppler shift.

### Understand the model
A moving observer encounters wavefronts more or less frequently. A moving source changes the spacing of emitted wavefronts in the medium. Approach increases the observed frequency; recession decreases it. These shifts change the heard pitch without changing the source’s own emitted frequency. Sound-speed formulas use motion relative to the medium, not just an unspecified relative speed.

For a stationary observer and a source approaching at speed v_s less than sound speed v, f_observed = fv/(v - v_s). A source moving away uses v + v_s in the denominator. When a source moves faster than sound, successive disturbances form a shock front and a receiver can hear a sonic boom as that front passes. The boom is not a single event produced only at the instant the source first crosses the sound speed.

### Equations and units
```text
source approaches stationary observer: f_obs = f v/(v - v_s)
source recedes: f_obs = f v/(v + v_s)
observer approaches stationary source: f_obs = f(v + v_o)/v
Mach number = source speed/sound speed
```

### Worked example
A 500 Hz siren moves toward a stationary listener at 20.0 m/s in still air. Use v = 343 m/s.

1. The source approaches, so emitted wavefronts ahead are closer together and the heard frequency increases.
2. f_obs = 500 × 343/(343 - 20.0) ≈ 531 Hz, treating 500 Hz as the assigned source value.
3. If the source instead recedes at the same speed, f_obs = 500 × 343/363 ≈ 472 Hz. The source still emits 500 Hz in its own description.

### Common mistakes
Source-motion and observer-motion formulas differ. State whether the air is still and whether the specified speed is relative to the medium. The elementary subsonic formula is not a sonic-boom model.

### Your turn
1. A listener approaches a stationary 600 Hz source at 10.0 m/s in still air. Find observed frequency using v = 343 m/s.
2. A source moves at 686 m/s where sound speed is 343 m/s. Find Mach number.

### Solutions and reasoning
**1.** f_obs = 600(343 + 10.0)/343 ≈ 617 Hz using the assigned source frequency.

**2.** Mach number is 686/343 = 2.00, so the source is supersonic.

## 8.9 Investigate and reflect

### Inquiry task
Observe a pulse on a gently stretched rope or use the wave explorer. Track the pulse and one marked point of the medium separately. Then compare standing-wave patterns in an ideal string and an air column. For a low-volume sound investigation, collect resonance or beat data and report room conditions, length measurement, and sound-speed assumptions. Discuss environmental noise and the limits of loudness comparisons without calibrated instruments.

### Evidence checklist
- State the question and a testable prediction.
- Identify independent, dependent, and controlled variables.
- Record units, resolution, repeated trials, and a justified uncertainty estimate.
- Use a labelled graph or table and explain slope or area when relevant.
- Compare the result with the model and identify specific limitations.
- Explain an improvement and connect the result to a practical application.
