# Energy: quick review

## 1 Energy and work
### Key ideas
- **Energy** can be stored, transferred, and changed from one form to another. Work transfers energy when a force acts through a displacement.
- For a constant force, use the component **parallel to the displacement**. The angle is between the force and displacement, rather than between the force and the floor in every situation.
- Work is positive when the force helps the motion, negative when it opposes the motion, and zero for a perpendicular force or zero displacement.
- Work and energy are **scalars**, measured in joules. A person can use chemical energy while doing zero mechanical work on a stationary object.
### Equations and units
```
W = Fd cos θ       Parallel force: W = Fd
1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²/s²
Near Earth: g = 9.81 m/s²
```
Convert grams to kg, centimetres to m, and kJ to J. Distinguish the work of one force from the **net work of all forces**.
### Check yourself
**Q1.** You push a stuck car horizontally with 300 N for 10.0 s, but it does not move. How much mechanical work do you do on the car? Explain why you may still feel tired.

**Q2.** A constant 50.0 N force pulls a crate through a horizontal displacement of 4.00 m. The force is directed 60.0° above the horizontal. Calculate the work done by this force.

**Q3.** A student lifts a 2.00 kg book vertically by 1.50 m at constant speed. Find the work done by the student, by gravity, and by the net force on the book.

## 2 Power, pulleys, and mechanical advantage
### Key ideas
- **Power** is the rate of energy transfer. Raising the same load through the same height in less time requires greater useful power.
- An ideal pulley trades a smaller effort force for a larger effort distance. It does not reduce input work below useful output work.
- For a simple ideal pulley, count the rope segments **supporting the moving load assembly**. A fixed pulley alone can change the pull direction without reducing the effort.
- Real machines transfer some input energy into thermal energy and sound. Compare useful output with total input over the same time interval.
### Equations and units
```
P = W/Δt = ΔE/Δt        1 W = 1 J/s
1 kW = 1000 W           1 hp ≈ 746 W
W_in = F_e d_e          W_out = F_l d_l
AMA = F_l/F_e           IMA = d_e/d_l
Ideal: W_in = W_out     η = (W_out/W_in) × 100%
```
F_e and d_e are effort force and distance; F_l and d_l are load force and distance. The symbol W denotes work, while the **unit W** denotes a watt. For a steady lift, useful output work is mgh.
### Check yourself
**Q4.** A motor lifts a 650 N load through 12.0 m in 11.0 s at steady speed. Find its useful output work, average output power in watts, and power in horsepower.

**Q5.** An ideal pulley has two rope segments supporting a 100 N load. The load rises 0.500 m at steady speed. Find effort force, effort distance, input work, and output work.

**Q6.** A real pulley lifts a 100 N load by 0.500 m. Its effort force is 60.0 N acting through 1.00 m. Find AMA, IMA, efficiency, and the input energy not delivered as useful lifting work.

## 3 Kinetic energy and net work
### Key ideas
- **Kinetic energy** depends on mass and speed squared. Doubling mass doubles kinetic energy; doubling speed quadruples it.
- Kinetic energy is nonnegative and gives no direction. Equal masses at the same speed have equal kinetic energy even if moving in opposite directions.
- The **net work** equals the change in kinetic energy. Work by one force equals that change only when the other forces contribute zero total work.
- On a level road, a constant braking force does negative work. With the same braking-force magnitude, stopping distance is proportional to initial speed squared.
### Equations and method
```
E_k = ½mv²
W_net = ΔE_k = ½m(v_f² − v_i²)
Stopping with constant braking force f: −fd = −½mv_i²
Convert km/h to m/s: divide by 3.6
```
Find the initial and final kinetic energies separately if that makes the signs clearer. A negative net work means speed decreases; it does not mean kinetic energy is negative.
### Check yourself
**Q7.** A 2.00 kg cart moves at 3.00 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy. What would its kinetic energy be at 6.00 m/s? How does reversing its direction at 3.00 m/s affect its kinetic energy?

**Q8.** A 1200 kg car slows from 72.0 km/h to 36.0 km/h on a level road. Find the net work done on it. Explain the sign.

**Q9.** The same constant horizontal braking force stops two identical cars, initially moving at v and 2v. Compare the magnitudes of the required net work and their braking distances. Ignore reaction time.

## 4 Potential energy and energy conservation
### Key ideas
- Near Earth's surface, gravitational potential energy depends on **vertical height** above a chosen zero. Only height changes affect the energy change.
- Mechanical energy is kinetic plus potential energy. It stays constant when friction and drag are negligible and no external agent adds or removes energy.
- Friction can convert mechanical energy into thermal energy. **Total energy is conserved** when all stores and transfers are included.
- Define the system before writing its balance. Count friction either as work on the object or as thermal energy gained within a larger system, consistently.
### Equations and model conditions
```
E_p = mgh        ΔE_p = mg(h_f − h_i)
Ideal: ½mv_i² + mgh_i = ½mv_f² + mgh_f
From rest, vertical drop Δh: v = √(2gΔh)
With thermal gain: E_mi + W_ext = E_mf + ΔE_th
```
The last line includes the object, Earth, and rubbing surfaces; assume no other transfers. Work can also store elastic potential energy in a compressed spring.
### Check yourself
**Q10.** A 1.00 kg book is held 0.500 m above a 1.00 m-high table. Find its gravitational potential energy relative to the tabletop and relative to the floor. Does changing the zero alter the energy needed to lift it from the table to this position?

**Q11.** A pendulum bob is released from rest 0.360 m vertically above its lowest position. Ignoring resistance, find its speed at the bottom. Would doubling its mass change this speed?

**Q12.** A 2.00 kg cart starts from rest 3.00 m above the bottom of a track. During descent, the cart–track–Earth system gains 10.0 J of thermal energy. With no other transfers, find the cart's bottom speed. Is mechanical energy conserved? Is total energy conserved?

## 5 Temperature, thermal energy, and heat
### Key ideas
- **Temperature** describes thermal state. For an ideal gas, average translational kinetic energy per particle is proportional to absolute temperature in kelvins.
- **Thermal energy** depends on the quantity and kind of material as well as its temperature and state. Equal temperatures do not imply equal internal energies.
- **Heat** is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. It flows spontaneously from hotter to colder objects; it is not a stored substance inside them.
- At thermal equilibrium, objects have the same temperature and no net heat transfer between them. Absolute zero is 0 K; the classical model does not imply that all quantum motion vanishes.
### Temperature scales
```
T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15
ΔT in kelvins = ΔT in Celsius degrees
Ideal-gas energy ratio: E_avg,2/E_avg,1 = T_2/T_1
```
Use kelvins for absolute-temperature ratios. Write K, without a degree sign. Do not add 273.15 to a **temperature change**.
### Check yourself
**Q13.** A drop and a bucket of water are both at 80.0°C. Compare their temperatures and thermal energies. When a hotter nail enters the bucket, which way does heat initially flow, and what becomes equal at equilibrium?

**Q14.** Convert 20.0°C to kelvins. Water then warms from 20.0°C to 60.0°C: give the temperature change in Celsius degrees and kelvins.

**Q15.** Helium atoms initially at 20.0°C have twice their initial average translational kinetic energy. Find the new temperature in kelvins and Celsius. Explain why 40.0°C is incorrect.

## 6 Heat transfer and insulation
### Key ideas
- **Conduction** transfers energy through microscopic interactions without bulk movement of material. A metal rod conducts energy along its length.
- **Convection** transfers energy through bulk motion of a fluid. Warmer, less dense fluid often rises as cooler, denser fluid moves in to replace it.
- **Radiation** transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and can cross a vacuum. The Sun transfers energy to Earth this way.
- An effective insulator reduces several transfer routes. A vacuum suppresses conduction and convection across a gap; reflective surfaces reduce radiation.
### Observations and fair comparisons
Metal and wood at the same room temperature can feel different because metal transfers energy away from your hand faster. Touch alone does not measure temperature reliably.

In an absorption investigation, keep water mass, initial temperature, container dimensions, lamp distance, and exposure time the same. Dark, matte surfaces often absorb more than shiny metallic surfaces under the test conditions; material and wavelength matter too.
### Check yourself
**Q16.** Identify the main transfer method in each case: (a) a spoon handle warms in hot soup; (b) heated water rises and circulates in a kettle; (c) sunlight reaches Earth through space. Which method can cross a vacuum?

**Q17.** A vacuum flask has a vacuum gap, reflective surfaces, and an insulating stopper. Explain how each feature reduces energy transfer. Does the vacuum eliminate radiation?

**Q18.** Land is warmer than the nearby sea during the day. Predict the idealized near-surface breeze and explain the circulation. How can the breeze reverse at night if the land becomes cooler than the sea?

## 7 Thermal calculations, efficiency, and graphs
### Key ideas
- **Specific heat capacity** is the energy needed to warm 1 kg of a substance by 1°C or 1 K. Higher c means a smaller temperature rise for the same energy and mass.
- Use Q = mcΔT for temperature changes without a phase change, with approximately constant c. Q is positive for heat entering the sample and negative for heat leaving it.
- In an insulated mixing model, heat gained plus heat lost is zero. Include every body that absorbs appreciable energy, including the container if required.
- Efficiency compares useful output with total input. A temperature-versus-time slope can give the power entering the water; heater input power can be greater.
### Equations and units
```
Q = mcΔT        ΔT = T_f − T_i
Water: c = 4200 J/(kg·°C)
Insulated mixing: ΣQ = 0
η = (E_useful/E_input) × 100%
P_water = mc(ΔT/Δt)       E_input = P_input Δt
```
Convert water mass to kg, time to seconds, and input power to watts. A temperature-time slope has units °C/s, not joules or watts.
### Check yourself
**Q19.** How much heat must enter 0.500 kg of water to warm it from 20.0°C to 60.0°C? How much heat must leave to return it to 20.0°C? Assume no phase change.

**Q20.** Mix 0.200 kg of water at 80.0°C with 0.300 kg at 20.0°C. Neglect heat exchange with the container and surroundings. Find the common final temperature and the heat gained by the initially cooler water.

**Q21.** A 200 W hot plate warms 0.300 kg of water from 20.0°C to 32.0°C in 120 s at an approximately constant rate. Find the temperature-time slope, average useful power into the water, and efficiency. Where does the remaining input energy go?

## 8 Answers: questions 1–12
### Work · Q1–3
**1.** W = Fd = (300)(0) = **0 J**. There is no car displacement. Your muscles still consume chemical energy and release thermal energy.

**2.** W = Fd cos θ = (50.0)(4.00) cos 60.0° = **+100 J**. Only the horizontal force component does work here.

**3.** At constant speed, the lifting force is mg. W_student = (2.00)(9.81)(1.50) = **+29.4 J**. W_gravity = **−29.4 J**; W_net = **0 J**. Kinetic energy stays constant.
### Power and machines · Q4–6
**4.** W_out = (650)(12.0) = **7.80 × 10³ J**. P_out = 7800/11.0 = **709 W**. In horsepower: 709.09/746 = **0.951 hp**.

**5.** F_e = 100/2 = **50.0 N**; d_e = (2)(0.500) = **1.00 m**. W_in = (50.0)(1.00) = **50.0 J**; W_out = (100)(0.500) = **50.0 J**. Half the effort requires twice the distance.

**6.** AMA = 100/60.0 = **1.67**; IMA = 1.00/0.500 = **2.00**. W_in = 60.0 J; W_out = 50.0 J. η = (50.0/60.0) × 100% = **83.3%**. The remaining **10.0 J** becomes thermal energy or other output.
### Kinetic energy · Q7–9
**7.** E_k = ½(2.00)(3.00)² = **9.00 J**. At 6.00 m/s: **36.0 J**, four times as much. Reversing at 3.00 m/s leaves **9.00 J**; kinetic energy has no direction.

**8.** v_i = 72.0/3.6 = 20.0 m/s; v_f = 36.0/3.6 = 10.0 m/s. W_net = ½(1200)(10.0² − 20.0²) = **−1.80 × 10⁵ J**. Negative work reduces kinetic energy.

**9.** At 2v, kinetic energy and required work magnitude are **four times** greater. Since fd = ½mv² with unchanged f, braking distance is also **four times** as long.
### Potential energy and conservation · Q10–12
**10.** Relative to the table: E_p = (1.00)(9.81)(0.500) = **4.91 J**. Relative to the floor: (1.00)(9.81)(1.500) = **14.7 J**. The lifting energy is **4.91 J** with either zero.

**11.** Set zero height at the bottom. mgh = ½mv² gives v = √[(2)(9.81)(0.360)] = **2.66 m/s**. Mass cancels: doubling it leaves the speed unchanged.

**12.** Initially, mgh = (2.00)(9.81)(3.00) = 58.86 J. Final E_k = 58.86 − 10.0 = 48.86 J. Thus v = √[(2)(48.86)/2.00] = **6.99 m/s**. Mechanical energy falls 10.0 J; **total energy is conserved**, including thermal energy.

## 9 Answers: questions 13–21
### Temperature and heat · Q13–15
**13.** Both have **the same temperature**; the bucket has more thermal energy because it contains more water. Heat flows **from the hotter nail to the water**. Equilibrium means equal temperatures, rather than equal internal energies.

**14.** T = 20.0 + 273.15 = **293.15 K** (293.2 K rounded). ΔT = 60.0 − 20.0 = **40.0°C = 40.0 K**. The offset cancels in temperature differences.

**15.** T_1 = 293.15 K. T_2 = 2T_1 = **586.30 K**, or **313.15°C** (about 586 K and 313°C). Energy ratios require kelvins; 40.0°C does not double the absolute temperature.
### Heat transfer · Q16–18
**16.** (a) **Conduction** through the spoon; (b) **convection** by circulating water; (c) **radiation** through space. Radiation can cross a vacuum. Conduction and convection need matter.

**17.** The vacuum suppresses conduction and convection across the gap. Reflective surfaces reduce radiation; the stopper reduces conduction through the neck, fluid exchange, and evaporation. **Radiation still crosses the vacuum**. Insulation is imperfect.

**18.** Daytime surface air moves **from sea to land** as warm air rises over land; return flow occurs higher up. At night, rising air over the warmer sea can draw surface air **from land to sea**.
### Thermal calculations and investigations · Q19–21
**19.** Q = (0.500)(4200)(60.0 − 20.0) = **+8.40 × 10⁴ J = +84.0 kJ**. Cooling back gives **Q = −84.0 kJ**: **84.0 kJ leaves** the water.

**20.** (0.200)c(80.0 − T_f) = (0.300)c(T_f − 20.0). Cancel c: T_f = [(0.200)(80.0) + (0.300)(20.0)]/0.500 = **44.0°C**. Cool water gains Q = (0.300)(4200)(24.0) = **3.02 × 10⁴ J**; hot water loses the same amount.

**21.** Slope = (32.0 − 20.0)/120 = **0.100°C/s**. P_water = (0.300)(4200)(0.100) = **126 W**. η = (126/200) × 100% = **63.0%**. Input is (200)(120) = 24 000 J; water gains 15 120 J. The remaining **8880 J** warms equipment or reaches the surroundings.
