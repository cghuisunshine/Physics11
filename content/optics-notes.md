# Light and Geometric Optics

## 1 Light and the electromagnetic spectrum
### The electromagnetic spectrum
Visible light occupies a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum. From long to short wavelengths, the main bands are **radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma ray**. In the textbook the visible range is about 380–760 nm, from violet to red. The boundaries are approximate.

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed c in vacuum. Higher frequency means shorter vacuum wavelength and higher energy per photon. A beam’s total energy also depends on how many photons it carries.
```
c = fλ ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum
1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m
```

### Example · Wavelength of orange light
Find the vacuum frequency of light with wavelength 600 nm.

**Worked answer**

Convert 600 nm to 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ m. Divide c by λ to get **5.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz**. A longer vacuum wavelength would give a lower frequency.

## 2 Rays, reflection, and plane-mirror images
### Draw a normal before measuring angles
A light ray represents the direction of light propagation. At the point of incidence, draw the **normal** perpendicular to the reflecting surface. The incident ray, normal, and reflected ray lie in one plane.
```
θᵢ = θᵣ
Measure both angles from the normal.
```
A smooth surface produces specular reflection. A rough surface reflects rays in many directions because different points have different local normals.

### A virtual image behind a plane mirror
Reflected rays diverge in front of the mirror. Trace them backward in straight lines to find the **virtual image**. It is upright and the same size as the object, at the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. A screen placed at the apparent image would receive no converging light there.

### Investigation 7-1A · Locating an image
Place a lit object in front of a clear sheet and line up an identical unlit marker behind it. If the object is 20.0 cm in front of the sheet, where should the marker go? Why might you see a faint second image?

**Worked answer**

In the thin-sheet approximation place the marker **20.0 cm behind** the reflecting surface. Check it from more than one viewing position. A faint second reflection can come from the back surface of the sheet. The two surfaces give slightly different ray paths.

## 3 Pinhole cameras and multiple reflections
### A pinhole produces a real image
Rays from the top and bottom of a scene cross at the hole and reach opposite parts of the screen. The image is inverted and real. Its size depends on the screen-to-hole distance compared with the object-to-hole distance.

### Small pinhole versus large pinhole
In the picture, light travels from the tree on the right through the opening to the screen on the left. Compare the two openings with the object and screen distances kept the same. Use the straight-line ray model: the hole lets light through but does not bend or focus it.

- **Small pinhole:** only a narrow bundle of rays from each point on the tree can pass through. That bundle reaches a small patch on the screen, so neighbouring object points produce little overlap. The image is sharper but dimmer because less light enters.
- **Large pinhole:** rays from the same point on the tree can pass through different parts of the opening. They continue along different straight paths and reach a larger patch on the screen. Patches from neighbouring points overlap, so edges and details merge into a fuzzy image. More light enters, making the image brighter.

Trace the two rays from the treetop in the lower drawing: they pass through different parts of the large hole and reach different places near the bottom of the screen. The same spreading into patches happens for every point on the tree. Both images remain inverted: light from the top reaches the lower screen and light from the bottom reaches the upper screen.

The picture calls the large-hole image “out of focus”; here the blur comes from overlapping patches of light, with no lens to bring each bundle to a point. Making an ordinary pinhole smaller reduces this overlap, but an extremely small hole can also blur the image by diffraction.

### Investigation 7-1B · Two mirrors and a periscope
Two angled mirrors can reflect each other’s images. With a centred object and an angle that divides 360° evenly, the ideal number of images is 360°/θ − 1. The object is counted separately. Parallel facing mirrors give a chain of repeated reflections. A periscope instead uses two parallel reflective faces at 45° to the tube to turn the light twice.

## 4 Concave and convex mirrors
### Start with the reflecting surface
A curved mirror is like part of a shiny spoon. Look at the side that reflects light:

- A **concave mirror curves inward**, like the inside of a spoon or a small cave.
- A **convex mirror bulges outward**, like the back of a spoon. Security mirrors are convex so they can show a wide view.

In the diagrams below, light arrives from the **left**. The left side is **in front of the mirror**, where light arrives and reflects. The shaded right side is **behind the mirror**. “Front” does not mean the right side of the page.

### Vertex, focus, and centre of curvature
Imagine cutting a small curved patch from a hollow sphere to make a **spherical mirror**. Its labels describe different things:

- **C — centre of curvature:** the centre of that imaginary sphere. C is a point in space, not a mark on the mirror.
- **V — vertex:** the middle point of the mirror's curved surface.
- **Principal axis:** the straight reference line through C and V.
- **F — principal focus:** the point where rays initially parallel to the axis meet after reflection, or appear to come from. The next two diagrams show the difference.

The **radius of curvature R** is the distance from V to C. The **focal length f** describes how far F is from V. For a spherical mirror, using rays close to the principal axis:
```
distance VF = |f| ≈ R/2
```
The bars in |f| mean the **positive distance**, without a sign. For example, if R = 40 cm, F is about 20 cm from V, halfway between V and C. Topic 6 introduces signed focal lengths.

### Concave: parallel rays come together
Follow the arrows: two parallel incoming rays strike different parts of the inward-curving mirror, then reflect back toward the left. The reflected rays **converge**, meaning they come together at F, in front of the mirror.

CONCAVE_FOCUS_FIGURE

This is a **real focus** because actual light passes through it. A screen placed there can receive a small bright spot from a distant point source. Here the order along the axis is **C — F — V**: C is the sphere's centre; F is the light's meeting point. They are different points.

### Convex: parallel rays spread apart
The outward-curving mirror makes the reflected rays **diverge**, meaning they spread apart as they return toward the left. They never actually meet in front of the mirror.

CONVEX_FOCUS_FIGURE

Extend the reflected rays backward using the **dashed lines**. Those extensions meet at F behind the mirror, so the light **appears to have come from F**. This is a **virtual focus**: the dashed lines are a drawing aid; light does not travel through the mirror along them. A screen placed at that F cannot catch a focused spot. Here the order is **V — F — C**, behind the mirror.

**Remember:** concave brings parallel rays together; convex spreads them apart. These statements are about rays arriving parallel to the principal axis. A nearby object's image need not be at F; Topic 5 shows its position.

### How well does the spherical model work?
For a spherical mirror, rays far from the axis can miss the common focus, blurring the spot. This is called **spherical aberration**. We use rays near the axis so that F is approximately halfway to C. A **parabolic mirror** has a different curve that brings rays parallel to its axis to the same focus in the ideal model.

### Investigation 7-2A · Measuring focal length
Why use a distant lamp when finding the focal length of a concave mirror? What is f if the sharp image of a very distant lamp is 18 cm from the vertex?

**Worked answer**

1. A very distant lamp sends rays to the mirror that are almost parallel. A concave mirror brings those rays together near F.
2. Move a screen in front of the mirror until the lamp's image is sharp. The screen is now approximately at F.
3. Measure from **V to the screen**, not from C. Here VF = 18 cm, so **f ≈ +18 cm** for this concave mirror.

The length unit is cm, as expected. As a model check, R ≈ 2f ≈ 36 cm, placing C twice as far from V as F. A closer lamp outside F forms its sharp image farther from the mirror than F, so using that screen distance as f would overestimate the focal length.

## 5 Drawing ray diagrams for mirrors
### Trace two rays from the tip
For a concave mirror, a ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through F. A ray through F reflects parallel to the axis. A ray directed through C hits the mirror along its local normal and retraces its path. A ray striking the vertex reflects at equal angles to the principal axis.

Where reflected rays cross, they form a **real image**. Where only their backward extensions cross, they form a **virtual image**. Draw extensions as dashed lines. For a convex mirror, a parallel incident ray reflects as if it came from the focus behind the mirror.

## 6 Mirror equation and magnification
### Two questions, two equations
A ray diagram shows roughly where an image forms. The **mirror equation** calculates its position. **Magnification** tells you whether the image is upright or inverted and how its height compares with the object's height.

Use the ideal spherical-mirror model with rays close to the principal axis. The objects in this section are real objects placed in front of the reflecting surface.

### Use signed distances
Start every distance measurement at **V, the mirror vertex**, and measure along the principal axis. The subscripts o and i mean **object** and **image**:

- **dₒ — object distance:** from V to the object. For a real object in front of the mirror, dₒ is positive.
- **dᵢ — image distance:** from V to the image. A real image in front has positive dᵢ; a virtual image behind has negative dᵢ.
- **f — focal length:** from V to F, with a sign. A concave mirror has positive f; a convex mirror has negative f.

These are **optics sign rules**, not a left/right coordinate rule. If you draw the mirror facing the other way, a real image in front still has positive dᵢ. For a concave mirror, the object position determines whether its image is real or virtual; the mirror type alone does not settle that.

### Calculate image position first
**One mirror equation works for both concave and convex mirrors:**
```
1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ
```
Use **f > 0 for concave** and **f < 0 for convex**. Keep the equation the same; the sign of f accounts for the mirror's shape. All three distances must use the **same unit**, such as cm. To find dᵢ, subtract 1/dₒ from both sides, then take the reciprocal:
```
1/dᵢ = 1/f − 1/dₒ
     = (dₒ − f)/(f × dₒ)

dᵢ = (f × dₒ)/(dₒ − f)
```
This rearranged form also works for **both mirror types**, provided dₒ ≠ f.

Further reading: [Why does one mirror equation work for both shapes?](mirror-equation-derivation.html)

**The reciprocal step matters:** if 1/dᵢ = 0.0400 cm⁻¹, then dᵢ = 1/0.0400 = 25.0 cm. The number 0.0400 is not the image distance. If the right side is zero, the reflected rays are parallel and there is no finite image distance; do not divide by zero.

Read the sign of your result: **positive dᵢ → real image in front**, which can be received on a screen; **negative dᵢ → virtual image behind**, located by backward ray extensions.

### Read the equation on a graph
Compare mirrors with the **same focal-length magnitude, 20.0 cm**. The solid curve uses **f = +20.0 cm for concave**; the dashed curve uses **f = −20.0 cm for convex**. Both are calculated from **dᵢ = (f × dₒ)/(dₒ − f)**. These are model curves, not measured data.

MIRROR_DISTANCE_GRAPH

Read **dₒ on the horizontal axis**, then go to the curve and read **dᵢ on the vertical axis**:

- **Concave, dₒ = 100 cm:** dᵢ = (+20.0 × 100)/(100 − 20.0) = **+25.0 cm**, a real image in front.
- **Convex, the same dₒ = 100 cm:** dᵢ = (−20.0 × 100)/(100 − (−20.0)) = **−16.7 cm**, a virtual image behind. Notice that subtracting negative f makes the denominator 120 cm.
- **Concave, dₒ = 10.0 cm:** dᵢ = **−20.0 cm**, a virtual image behind. This object is inside F.
- **Concave, dₒ = 20.0 cm = f:** there is **no finite image distance**, so its two curve branches do not join. The grey vertical line marks this object position. The convex curve remains continuous there: its f is −20.0 cm, so dₒ − f is not zero.

For a convex mirror with a real object, f × dₒ is negative and dₒ − f is positive. Therefore **dᵢ is always negative**: the image is virtual. For a concave mirror, dᵢ can be positive or negative depending on whether the object is outside or inside F.

This graph plots two **distances**. It is not a picture of a light ray or the mirror's shape. Large concave-mirror image distances near dₒ = f extend beyond the plotted range.

### Then describe the image with magnification
Let **hₒ** be the object's height and **hᵢ** the image's signed height, both measured perpendicular to the axis. Take an upright object as positive: an image above the axis has positive height and an inverted image below has negative height.
```
m = hᵢ/hₒ = −dᵢ/dₒ
hᵢ = m × hₒ
```
Magnification m has **no units**, because it is a ratio of two lengths. Read its sign and size separately:

- **Sign gives orientation:** m > 0 means upright; m < 0 means inverted.
- **Magnitude gives relative height:** |m| < 1 means smaller; |m| = 1 means the same height; |m| > 1 means larger.

For example, **m = −0.250** means *inverted and one quarter as tall*. **m = +2.0** means *upright and twice as tall*. A minus sign describes orientation, not a physically negative size. Do not confuse the sign of dᵢ (image location) with the sign of m (image orientation).

The **same magnification equation also works for both mirrors**. For the convex case on the graph, m = −(−16.7)/100 ≈ **+0.167**: the image is upright and about one sixth as tall. For the concave case at the same object distance, m = −(+25.0)/100 = **−0.250**: inverted and one quarter as tall.

MIRROR_EQUATION_SOLUTION_FIGURE

In this **ray diagram**, the upright object is 100 cm from V and the inverted image is 25.0 cm from V. The two reflected rays cross at the image tip. Compare the arrow heights: the image is one quarter as tall, matching m = −25.0/100 = −0.250. Every horizontal distance starts at V.

### Sample 7.2.1 · Where does the image form?
A concave mirror of focal length 20.0 cm is 1.00 m from an object. Find image distance and magnification.

**Worked answer**

**1. Choose the principle and assign signs.** The mirror equation applies to this concave mirror in the small-angle model. Its focal length is positive, and the real object distance is positive. Convert to a common unit before calculating:
```
f = +20.0 cm
dₒ = 1.00 m = 1.00 × 10² cm
```
**2. Solve for image distance.** Subtract reciprocals, then invert the result:
```
1/dᵢ = 1/f − 1/dₒ
     = 1/(20.0 cm) − 1/(100 cm)
     = 0.0500 cm⁻¹ − 0.0100 cm⁻¹
     = 0.0400 cm⁻¹

dᵢ = 1/(0.0400 cm⁻¹) = +25.0 cm
```
The positive sign places a **real image 25.0 cm in front of V**. Both the object and this image are on the reflecting side of the mirror.

**3. Calculate magnification.** Keep the minus sign in the formula:
```
m = −dᵢ/dₒ
  = −(+25.0 cm)/(100 cm)
  = −0.250
```
The image is **inverted** because m is negative, and its height is **0.250 times the object's height**. No object height was supplied, so an image height in centimetres cannot be calculated.

**4. Check the answer.** Substitution gives 1/20.0 = 1/100 + 1/25.0, with every term in cm⁻¹. Also, C is 2f = 40.0 cm in front of V. The object is beyond C, so Topic 5 predicts a real, inverted, smaller image between F and C. The calculated 20.0 cm < 25.0 cm < 40.0 cm agrees.

## 7 Refractive index and Snell’s law
### Refractive index tells you the speed
Light travels at **c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum**. In a transparent material it travels at speed v. The **refractive index n** compares these speeds:
```
n = c/v          so          v = c/n
```
The units cancel, so **n has no units**. A larger n means a smaller speed. Air has n ≈ 1.00; the glass in our example has n = 1.50, so its light speed is approximately (3.00 × 10⁸)/1.50 = **2.00 × 10⁸ m/s**. An index of 1.50 means the speed is c/1.50.

### What changes when light enters another medium?
At a **stationary boundary**, transmitted light keeps the **same frequency f**, measured in hertz (Hz). Here f means frequency, not the mirror focal length used in Topic 6. Speed v and wavelength λ can change:
```
v = fλ          so          λ = v/f
```
If the speed decreases while frequency stays constant, the wavelength becomes shorter. If the speed increases, the wavelength becomes longer. At an oblique angle, the ray can also change direction: this is **refraction**.

### Draw the normal before measuring angles
The **boundary** is the surface separating the materials. The **normal** is an imaginary line perpendicular to that surface at the point where the ray crosses it.

- **Medium 1** is where the light starts: index n₁ and incident angle θ₁.
- **Medium 2** is where the light enters: index n₂ and refracted angle θ₂.
- Measure **both angles from the normal**, not from the surface. A ray along the normal has angle 0°.

REFRACTION_DIRECTIONS_FIGURE

In the left panel, light slows down in glass and bends **toward the normal**: θ₂ is smaller. In the right panel, the same path is reversed: light speeds up in air and bends **away from the normal**. The medium numbers follow the direction of travel; glass becomes medium 1 on the return trip.

### One Snell’s law works in both directions
For refraction between two transparent media:
```
n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

sin θ₂ = (n₁/n₂) sin θ₁
θ₂ = sin⁻¹[(n₁/n₂) sin θ₁]
```
Use **degree mode** when your angles are in degrees. The calculator's **sin⁻¹** means inverse sine (arcsine), not 1/sin.

At oblique incidence, **higher n → slower → toward the normal**; **lower n → faster → away from the normal**, provided a refracted ray exists. At normal incidence, θ₁ = 0° gives θ₂ = 0°: speed and wavelength can change without bending the ray. If the calculated sine exceeds 1, no real refracted angle exists; Topic 9 explains total internal reflection.

### Read the angle change on a graph

### Example · Air into glass
A ray enters glass of index 1.50 from air at 35.0° to the normal. Find its angle in glass.

**Worked answer**

**1. Identify the media and predict the bend.** The ray starts in air, so n₁ ≈ 1.00, and enters glass, so n₂ = 1.50. Because n₂ > n₁, predict θ₂ < 35.0°. The given 35.0° is already measured from the normal.

**2. Apply Snell’s law and isolate the unknown sine.**
```
n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
sin θ₂ = (n₁/n₂) sin θ₁
       = (1.00/1.50) sin 35.0°
       ≈ 0.3824
```
**3. Take inverse sine to find the angle.**
```
θ₂ = sin⁻¹(0.3824)
θ₂ ≈ 22.5°
```
The refracted ray travels in the glass at **22.5° to the normal**. The intermediate value 0.3824 is a sine ratio, not an angle.

**4. Check.** The angle decreased, as predicted. Substitution gives n₁ sin θ₁ ≈ 0.574 and n₂ sin θ₂ ≈ 0.574 using the unrounded angle. The refractive indices and sines are unitless; the final angle is in degrees. The diagram and graph show the same result.

## 8 Reversibility, apparent depth, and measuring n
### Why a submerged object appears shallower
Rays leaving water for air bend away from the normal. Your eye traces those rays back to a shallower apparent position. To aim a light ray at a fish, aim along the line of sight. Refraction sends the light back along that path. A straight-moving object must instead be directed toward the actual, deeper fish.

A ray through a glass block with parallel faces and the same medium outside both sides emerges parallel to its incident direction. Oblique incidence usually gives a sideways offset.

### Investigation 7-3A · Find n from a graph
Measure angles in air and in the material. Why graph sin θ(air) vertically against sin θ(material) horizontally? If the graph passes through (0.200, 0.300), estimate the material’s index.

**Worked answer**

Snell’s law gives a straight line with slope n(material)/n(air). The slope is 0.300/0.200 = 1.50, so **n ≈ 1.50** for air with n ≈ 1.00. Use a best-fit line through several points to reduce the effect of measurement scatter. The raw angle ratio approximates the index ratio only at small angles.

## 9 Critical angle and total internal reflection
### A ray can remain inside the higher-index material
Total internal reflection requires travel from a **higher index** n₁ to a **lower index** n₂ and an incidence angle larger than the critical angle θc. At θc the ideal refracted ray is along the boundary.
```
sin θc = n₂/n₁     when n₁ > n₂
```
At smaller incidence angles, some light usually transmits and some reflects. Optical fibres guide light in a higher-index core surrounded by lower-index cladding. Right-angle prisms can turn a ray by total internal reflection.

### Investigation 7-3B · Water to air
Estimate θc for water with n = 1.33. A ray in water hits the surface at 55° to the normal. Does any refracted ray propagate into air in the ideal model?

**Worked answer**

θc = sin⁻¹(1.00/1.33) ≈ **48.8°**. The ray at 55° is above the critical angle, so it undergoes total internal reflection.

## 10 Dispersion and rainbows
### Why a prism separates colours
In ordinary glass, shorter visible wavelengths generally have larger refractive indices and bend more at oblique incidence. This wavelength dependence is called **dispersion**. A prism separates the components of white light. A parallel-sided slab has no net angular deviation for each colour when the outside medium is the same on both sides.

### Rainbows in raindrops
Look toward rain with the Sun behind you. Light refracts and disperses on entering the drops, reflects internally, then refracts again on exit. The primary bow has red outside and violet inside. The fainter secondary bow involves two internal reflections and reverses the order.

### Example · Colour when light enters water
A red laser has wavelength 650 nm in air. Find its wavelength in water of index 1.33. Does the light become blue-green?

**Worked answer**

λ(water) ≈ 650/1.33 = **489 nm**. The wavelength changes because the light slows. The frequency stays fixed, so the red light still looks red. Light with 489 nm wavelength **in air** would have a different frequency.

## 11 Polarization and glare
### A direction for the electric field
Light is transverse. A linear polarizer transmits the electric-field component along its transmission axis. Two ideal polarizers with axes at right angles block a beam after the first has selected one linear polarization. A calcite crystal can split light into two rays with different polarizations.

### Investigation 7-3C · Rotate the filter
Why can sunglasses with a vertical transmission axis reduce glare from a road? How would you test a camera polarizer at the edge of a pond?

**Worked answer**

Reflections from horizontal surfaces can be strongly **horizontally polarized**. A vertical transmission axis suppresses much of that glare. Rotate the camera filter while watching the surface. At the angle that dims the reflection, detail beneath the surface may become clearer.

## 12 Thin lenses and principal rays
### Converging and diverging lenses
For ordinary glass in air, a lens thicker in the centre is typically **converging**, and one thinner in the centre is typically **diverging**. A thin lens has a focus on each side. To draw a ray diagram, treat the ray as changing direction at the centre plane. The actual refraction occurs at both surfaces.

- For a converging lens, an incoming ray parallel to the axis passes through the far focus. A ray through the near focus leaves parallel.
- For a diverging lens, a parallel incoming ray leaves as if it came from the near focus. A ray aimed at the far focus leaves parallel.
- A ray through the optical centre of a thin lens travels approximately straight.

### Investigation 7-4A · Comparing lens shapes
A ray box sends parallel rays through a convex cylindrical lens and then through a concave cylindrical lens in separate trials. What happens in each trial? What can happen if the two lenses are placed together?

**Worked answer**

The convex lens converges rays in the curved cross-section. The concave lens diverges them. In contact, lens powers add. If the powers are equal and opposite, the combination has approximately zero net power. Separating the lenses changes the bundle entering the second one.

## 13 Thin-lens equation and image size
### Same algebra, different image side
The thin-lens equation uses the same signed form as the mirror equation.
```
1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ         m = −dᵢ/dₒ
Converging lens: f > 0   Diverging lens: f < 0
```
For a lens and a real object, a positive dᵢ places the real image on the **opposite side**. Negative dᵢ places the virtual image on the **object side**. For a converging lens, moving a real object from beyond 2f to f moves the real image farther away. At 2f the image is life-size. Inside f it is virtual, upright, and enlarged. A diverging lens with a real object makes an upright, reduced virtual image.

### Example · Image on a screen
A 4.0 cm object is 30.0 cm from a converging lens of focal length 20.0 cm. Find where to put the screen and predict the image height.

**Worked answer**
```
1/dᵢ = 1/20.0 − 1/30.0 = 1/60.0 cm⁻¹
dᵢ = +60.0 cm, on the opposite side
m = −60.0/30.0 = −2.00
hᵢ = −8.0 cm
```
The image is real and inverted, with a height of **8.0 cm**. The negative sign gives its orientation.

### Investigation 7-4B · Predict and test
Focus a distant object on a screen to estimate f. Then place a nearby object at exactly 2f and find the sharp image. What changes when the object is moved inside f?

**Worked answer**

At 2f, expect a real, inverted, life-size image at 2f on the far side. Inside f, no screen position produces the virtual image. Look through the lens toward the object to see an upright magnified view. Compare measured distances with the lens equation.

## 14 The eye and corrective lenses
### Accommodation and the retina
The cornea provides much of the eye’s focusing power. The eye lens adjusts its shape to bring near or far objects into focus on the retina. The iris changes the pupil size to control light entry.

- With **myopia** (nearsightedness), rays from a distant object would focus in front of the retina. A diverging corrective lens reduces their convergence.
- With **hypermetropia** (farsightedness), light from a near object may focus behind the retina. A converging lens adds focusing power.
- **Astigmatism** can arise when different directions have different curvatures. A cylindrical correction changes power by direction.

### Example · Choose the correcting lens
An uncorrected eye focuses parallel light in front of its retina. Which kind of lens helps, and how does it change the rays?

**Worked answer**

Use a **diverging lens** for the nearsighted eye. It spreads the parallel rays slightly before they enter the eye. The eye then brings them together farther back, at the retina when the correction is suitable.

## 15 Cameras, projectors, microscopes, and telescopes
### Match the instrument to the image
A **camera** forms a real image on a sensor. For distant objects, the sensor is near the focal plane. To focus closer with a fixed focal length, increase the lens-to-sensor distance. A **projector** places a slide between f and 2f to form a larger real image on a distant screen. A **magnifying glass** holds the object inside f, producing a larger upright virtual image.

A **compound microscope** uses an objective to make a real, enlarged intermediate image. The eyepiece magnifies this image as a virtual image. An **astronomical telescope** uses its objective lens or mirror to form a real image of a distant object near its focal plane. The eyepiece increases the apparent angular size. Binoculars commonly use prisms to fold the light path and orient the image.

### Example · A life-size photograph
With a simple convex lens, where must a small object and a sensor be placed to make an image the same size as the object?

**Worked answer**

For a life-size real image, |m| = 1 and dᵢ = dₒ. Then 1/f = 2/dₒ. Place the object and sensor at **2f on opposite sides** of the lens. The image on the sensor is inverted.
