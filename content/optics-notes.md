# Light and Geometric Optics

## 1 Light and the electromagnetic spectrum
### The electromagnetic spectrum
Visible light occupies a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum. From long to short wavelengths, the main bands are **radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma ray**. In the textbook the visible range is about 380–760 nm, from violet to red. The boundaries are approximate.

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed c in vacuum. Higher frequency means shorter vacuum wavelength and higher energy per photon. A beam’s total energy also depends on how many photons it carries.
```
c = fλ ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum
1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m
```

### Example · Wavelength of orange light
Find the vacuum frequency of light with wavelength 600 nm.

**Worked answer**

Convert 600 nm to 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ m. Divide c by λ to get **5.00 × 10¹⁴ Hz**. A longer vacuum wavelength would give a lower frequency.

## 2 Rays, reflection, and plane-mirror images
### Draw a normal before measuring angles
A light ray represents the direction of light propagation. At the point of incidence, draw the **normal** perpendicular to the reflecting surface. The incident ray, normal, and reflected ray lie in one plane.
```
θᵢ = θᵣ
Measure both angles from the normal.
```
A smooth surface produces specular reflection. A rough surface reflects rays in many directions because different points have different local normals.

### A virtual image behind a plane mirror
Reflected rays diverge in front of the mirror. Trace them backward in straight lines to find the **virtual image**. It is upright and the same size as the object, at the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front. A screen placed at the apparent image would receive no converging light there.

### Investigation 7-1A · Locating an image
Place a lit object in front of a clear sheet and line up an identical unlit marker behind it. If the object is 20.0 cm in front of the sheet, where should the marker go? Why might you see a faint second image?

**Worked answer**

In the thin-sheet approximation place the marker **20.0 cm behind** the reflecting surface. Check it from more than one viewing position. A faint second reflection can come from the back surface of the sheet. The two surfaces give slightly different ray paths.

## 3 Pinhole cameras and multiple reflections
### A pinhole produces a real image
Rays from the top and bottom of a scene cross at the hole and reach opposite parts of the screen. The image is inverted and real. Its size depends on the screen-to-hole distance compared with the object-to-hole distance. Smaller holes let less light through. Extremely small holes can blur the image by diffraction.

### Investigation 7-1B · Two mirrors and a periscope
Two angled mirrors can reflect each other’s images. With a centred object and an angle that divides 360° evenly, the ideal number of images is 360°/θ − 1. The object is counted separately. Parallel facing mirrors give a chain of repeated reflections. A periscope instead uses two parallel reflective faces at 45° to the tube to turn the light twice.

### Example · Mirror height and image count
A student is 1.76 m tall. What is the minimum correctly positioned plane-mirror height to see their full body? How many images does a symmetric 60° mirror pair produce?

**Worked answer**

The limiting rays reflect halfway from the eye to the head and halfway from the eye to the feet. Their reflection points are separated by **0.880 m**, independent of distance to the mirror. The symmetric pair gives **360°/60° − 1 = 5 images** in the ideal model.

## 4 Concave and convex mirrors
### Vertex, focus, and centre of curvature
The **vertex V** is where the principal axis meets the mirror. For a spherical mirror, the axis also passes through the **centre of curvature C**. The radius R is the distance VC. Near the axis, the focus F lies about halfway between C and V.
```
|f| ≈ R/2 for a spherical mirror and small ray angles
```
A **concave mirror** can bring an axial parallel bundle to a real focus in front. A **convex mirror** spreads the bundle. Its reflected rays appear to come from a virtual focus behind the mirror. A parabolic mirror brings rays parallel to its axis to the same focus. A spherical mirror can show spherical aberration for rays far from the axis.

### Investigation 7-2A · Measuring focal length
Why use a distant lamp when finding the focal length of a concave mirror? What is f if the sharp image of a very distant lamp is 18 cm from the vertex?

**Worked answer**

The incoming rays from a distant point are almost parallel, so the screen is approximately at the focus and **f ≈ 18 cm**. A closer lamp still gives a real image if it is outside f, but that image is farther from the mirror than the focal plane.

## 5 Drawing ray diagrams for mirrors
### Trace two rays from the tip
For a concave mirror, a ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through F. A ray through F reflects parallel to the axis. A ray directed through C hits the mirror along its local normal and retraces its path. A ray striking the vertex reflects at equal angles to the principal axis.

Where reflected rays cross, they form a **real image**. Where only their backward extensions cross, they form a **virtual image**. Draw extensions as dashed lines. For a convex mirror, a parallel incident ray reflects as if it came from the focus behind the mirror.

### Investigation 7-2B · Image positions
Put an object beyond C, at C, between C and F, at F, and inside F. Predict the image in each case before moving a screen. Then explain why a convex security mirror gives a reduced image.

**Worked answer**

- **Beyond C** gives a real, inverted, reduced image between C and F.
- **At C** gives a real, inverted, same-size image at C.
- **Between C and F** gives a real, inverted, enlarged image beyond C.
- **At F** gives parallel reflected rays from each object point. There is no finite image.
- **Inside F** gives an upright, enlarged virtual image behind the mirror.
- A **convex mirror** gives an upright, reduced virtual image for a real object. Its backward extensions cross between the vertex and the virtual focus.

## 6 Mirror equation and magnification
### Use signed distances
Measure distances from the mirror vertex. For a real object, dₒ is positive. Use positive f for a converging concave mirror and negative f for a diverging convex mirror.
```
1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ
m = hᵢ/hₒ = −dᵢ/dₒ
```
A positive dᵢ gives a real image in front of a mirror. A negative dᵢ gives a virtual image behind it. Negative magnification means inverted and positive magnification means upright. Compare |m| with 1 to describe size.

### Sample 7.2.1 · Where does the image form?
A concave mirror of focal length 20.0 cm is 1.00 m from an object. Find image distance and magnification.

**Worked answer**
```
dₒ = 100 cm
1/dᵢ = 1/20.0 − 1/100 = 0.0400 cm⁻¹
dᵢ = +25.0 cm
m = −25.0/100 = −0.250
```
The image is real, in front of the mirror, inverted, and one quarter the object height. Its position between f and 2f agrees with the diagram for a distant object.

## 7 Refractive index and Snell’s law
### Speed changes at a boundary
The **index of refraction n** is c divided by the speed of light in a material. At a stationary boundary the frequency stays the same. A wave that slows down has a shorter wavelength.
```
n = c/v                  λ = v/f
n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
```
Measure θ₁ and θ₂ from the normal. Light bends **toward the normal** when it enters a higher-index medium at oblique incidence. It bends **away from the normal** on entering a lower-index medium if a transmitted ray exists. At normal incidence it continues straight.

### Example · Air into glass
A ray enters glass of index 1.50 from air at 35.0° to the normal. Find its angle in glass.

**Worked answer**
```
sin θ₂ = (1.00/1.50) sin 35.0° = 0.3824
θ₂ ≈ 22.5°
```
Use degree mode. The smaller angle agrees with bending toward the normal.

## 8 Reversibility, apparent depth, and measuring n
### Why a submerged object appears shallower
Rays leaving water for air bend away from the normal. Your eye traces those rays back to a shallower apparent position. To aim a light ray at a fish, aim along the line of sight. Refraction sends the light back along that path. A straight-moving object must instead be directed toward the actual, deeper fish.

A ray through a glass block with parallel faces and the same medium outside both sides emerges parallel to its incident direction. Oblique incidence usually gives a sideways offset.

### Investigation 7-3A · Find n from a graph
Measure angles in air and in the material. Why graph sin θ(air) vertically against sin θ(material) horizontally? If the graph passes through (0.200, 0.300), estimate the material’s index.

**Worked answer**

Snell’s law gives a straight line with slope n(material)/n(air). The slope is 0.300/0.200 = 1.50, so **n ≈ 1.50** for air with n ≈ 1.00. Use a best-fit line through several points to reduce the effect of measurement scatter. The raw angle ratio approximates the index ratio only at small angles.

## 9 Critical angle and total internal reflection
### A ray can remain inside the higher-index material
Total internal reflection requires travel from a **higher index** n₁ to a **lower index** n₂ and an incidence angle larger than the critical angle θc. At θc the ideal refracted ray is along the boundary.
```
sin θc = n₂/n₁     when n₁ > n₂
```
At smaller incidence angles, some light usually transmits and some reflects. Optical fibres guide light in a higher-index core surrounded by lower-index cladding. Right-angle prisms can turn a ray by total internal reflection.

### Investigation 7-3B · Water to air
Estimate θc for water with n = 1.33. A ray in water hits the surface at 55° to the normal. Does any refracted ray propagate into air in the ideal model?

**Worked answer**

θc = sin⁻¹(1.00/1.33) ≈ **48.8°**. The ray at 55° is above the critical angle, so it undergoes total internal reflection.

## 10 Dispersion and rainbows
### Why a prism separates colours
In ordinary glass, shorter visible wavelengths generally have larger refractive indices and bend more at oblique incidence. This wavelength dependence is called **dispersion**. A prism separates the components of white light. A parallel-sided slab has no net angular deviation for each colour when the outside medium is the same on both sides.

### Rainbows in raindrops
Look toward rain with the Sun behind you. Light refracts and disperses on entering the drops, reflects internally, then refracts again on exit. The primary bow has red outside and violet inside. The fainter secondary bow involves two internal reflections and reverses the order.

### Example · Colour when light enters water
A red laser has wavelength 650 nm in air. Find its wavelength in water of index 1.33. Does the light become blue-green?

**Worked answer**

λ(water) ≈ 650/1.33 = **489 nm**. The wavelength changes because the light slows. The frequency stays fixed, so the red light still looks red. Light with 489 nm wavelength **in air** would have a different frequency.

## 11 Polarization and glare
### A direction for the electric field
Light is transverse. A linear polarizer transmits the electric-field component along its transmission axis. Two ideal polarizers with axes at right angles block a beam after the first has selected one linear polarization. A calcite crystal can split light into two rays with different polarizations.

### Investigation 7-3C · Rotate the filter
Why can sunglasses with a vertical transmission axis reduce glare from a road? How would you test a camera polarizer at the edge of a pond?

**Worked answer**

Reflections from horizontal surfaces can be strongly **horizontally polarized**. A vertical transmission axis suppresses much of that glare. Rotate the camera filter while watching the surface. At the angle that dims the reflection, detail beneath the surface may become clearer.

## 12 Thin lenses and principal rays
### Converging and diverging lenses
For ordinary glass in air, a lens thicker in the centre is typically **converging**, and one thinner in the centre is typically **diverging**. A thin lens has a focus on each side. To draw a ray diagram, treat the ray as changing direction at the centre plane. The actual refraction occurs at both surfaces.

- For a converging lens, an incoming ray parallel to the axis passes through the far focus. A ray through the near focus leaves parallel.
- For a diverging lens, a parallel incoming ray leaves as if it came from the near focus. A ray aimed at the far focus leaves parallel.
- A ray through the optical centre of a thin lens travels approximately straight.

### Investigation 7-4A · Comparing lens shapes
A ray box sends parallel rays through a convex cylindrical lens and then through a concave cylindrical lens in separate trials. What happens in each trial? What can happen if the two lenses are placed together?

**Worked answer**

The convex lens converges rays in the curved cross-section. The concave lens diverges them. In contact, lens powers add. If the powers are equal and opposite, the combination has approximately zero net power. Separating the lenses changes the bundle entering the second one.

## 13 Thin-lens equation and image size
### Same algebra, different image side
The thin-lens equation uses the same signed form as the mirror equation.
```
1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ         m = −dᵢ/dₒ
Converging lens: f > 0   Diverging lens: f < 0
```
For a lens and a real object, a positive dᵢ places the real image on the **opposite side**. Negative dᵢ places the virtual image on the **object side**. For a converging lens, moving a real object from beyond 2f to f moves the real image farther away. At 2f the image is life-size. Inside f it is virtual, upright, and enlarged. A diverging lens with a real object makes an upright, reduced virtual image.

### Example · Image on a screen
A 4.0 cm object is 30.0 cm from a converging lens of focal length 20.0 cm. Find where to put the screen and predict the image height.

**Worked answer**
```
1/dᵢ = 1/20.0 − 1/30.0 = 1/60.0 cm⁻¹
dᵢ = +60.0 cm, on the opposite side
m = −60.0/30.0 = −2.00
hᵢ = −8.0 cm
```
The image is real and inverted, with a height of **8.0 cm**. The negative sign gives its orientation.

### Investigation 7-4B · Predict and test
Focus a distant object on a screen to estimate f. Then place a nearby object at exactly 2f and find the sharp image. What changes when the object is moved inside f?

**Worked answer**

At 2f, expect a real, inverted, life-size image at 2f on the far side. Inside f, no screen position produces the virtual image. Look through the lens toward the object to see an upright magnified view. Compare measured distances with the lens equation.

## 14 The eye and corrective lenses
### Accommodation and the retina
The cornea provides much of the eye’s focusing power. The eye lens adjusts its shape to bring near or far objects into focus on the retina. The iris changes the pupil size to control light entry.

- With **myopia** (nearsightedness), rays from a distant object would focus in front of the retina. A diverging corrective lens reduces their convergence.
- With **hypermetropia** (farsightedness), light from a near object may focus behind the retina. A converging lens adds focusing power.
- **Astigmatism** can arise when different directions have different curvatures. A cylindrical correction changes power by direction.

### Example · Choose the correcting lens
An uncorrected eye focuses parallel light in front of its retina. Which kind of lens helps, and how does it change the rays?

**Worked answer**

Use a **diverging lens** for the nearsighted eye. It spreads the parallel rays slightly before they enter the eye. The eye then brings them together farther back, at the retina when the correction is suitable.

## 15 Cameras, projectors, microscopes, and telescopes
### Match the instrument to the image
A **camera** forms a real image on a sensor. For distant objects, the sensor is near the focal plane. To focus closer with a fixed focal length, increase the lens-to-sensor distance. A **projector** places a slide between f and 2f to form a larger real image on a distant screen. A **magnifying glass** holds the object inside f, producing a larger upright virtual image.

A **compound microscope** uses an objective to make a real, enlarged intermediate image. The eyepiece magnifies this image as a virtual image. An **astronomical telescope** uses its objective lens or mirror to form a real image of a distant object near its focal plane. The eyepiece increases the apparent angular size. Binoculars commonly use prisms to fold the light path and orient the image.

### Example · A life-size photograph
With a simple convex lens, where must a small object and a sensor be placed to make an image the same size as the object?

**Worked answer**

For a life-size real image, |m| = 1 and dᵢ = dₒ. Then 1/f = 2/dₒ. Place the object and sensor at **2f on opposite sides** of the lens. The image on the sensor is inverted.
